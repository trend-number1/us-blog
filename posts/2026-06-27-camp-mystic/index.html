Camp Mystic Bankruptcy: What Families Are Fighting For

June 27, 2026 12:03 Trend Insights

Can a bankruptcy filing erase accountability for the deaths of 28 people? That is the question now haunting the families of Camp Mystic, the nearly century-old Texas summer camp that filed for Chapter 11 protection just days before the one-year anniversary of one of the deadliest flash floods in Texas history.

The Camp Mystic bankruptcy has reignited grief and anger across Texas Hill Country. For the parents who lost daughters along the Guadalupe River, the legal maneuver feels less like financial reorganization and more like an escape hatch.

Quick Summary: Camp Mystic, where 28 people died in the July 4, 2025 floods, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on June 24, 2026. The filing pauses pending wrongful death lawsuits. Families and their attorneys call it an attempt to avoid responsibility, vowing to keep fighting for justice.

What Happened at Camp Mystic

Camp Mystic was an all-girls Christian sleepaway camp founded in 1926, making it one of Texas’ oldest and most well-known girls camps. It sat along the Guadalupe River in Kerr County, deep in the Texas Hill Country.

On July 4, 2025, the Guadalupe River flooded catastrophically. The disaster killed 28 people connected to the camp: 25 young campers, two 18-year-old counselors, and the camp’s co-executive director, Richard “Dick” Eastland.

The tragedy extended far beyond the camp itself. More than 100 people died across Texas Hill Country in the same flooding event, making it one of the region’s worst natural disasters in living memory.

The scale of loss was staggering. One of the missing, 8-year-old camper Cile Steward, was swept away in the Guadalupe River, and her body has yet to be recovered — a wound that remains open for her family.

But what came next turned natural tragedy into a fight over human responsibility.

A Scathing Report on What Went Wrong

Earlier in June 2026, state investigators released a 115-page report that faulted Camp Mystic on multiple fronts. The findings were blunt and damning.

According to the report, the camp fell short on:

  • Inadequate advance emergency planning
  • Insufficient storm preparation
  • Failed or delayed evacuations
  • Poor incident management

Perhaps the most striking detail: the hectic task of evacuating the entire camp fell on the shoulders of just three men — the camp’s co-owner, his son, and a security guard. Dick Eastland, the co-owner, died during the floods.

“The lessons to be learned from the camp’s inadequate emergency planning and response are worthy of careful study for opportunities to avoid similar future tragedies,” the report’s authors wrote.

That phrase — could have been avoided — has become the rallying cry for grieving families. In a Dateline exclusive, eight mothers who lost their daughters said together that the tragedy could have “100%” been averted.

If the deaths were preventable, families ask, then who pays the price? The bankruptcy filing complicates that answer.

Inside the Chapter 11 Bankruptcy Filing

On Wednesday, June 24, 2026, the company operating Camp Mystic filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas in Houston. The filing included Camp Mystic, LLC, along with three other affiliated entities.

The financial picture painted in court documents is grim. Here is how the numbers break down across the filing:

ItemAmount
Total debts / liabilities$10 million to $50 million
Total assets$1 million to $10 million
Lives lost28 people
Year founded1926

Court filings also noted that some assets “pose a threat of imminent and identifiable hazard to public health or safety,” with perishable goods and weather-exposed property requiring urgent attention.

Chapter 11 does not mean the camp is shutting down its legal existence — it means reorganization. And that is exactly what alarms the families.

Why does that distinction matter so much? Because of timing.

Why Families Are Fighting Back

Here is the crux of the controversy: filing for Chapter 11 bankruptcy pauses pending litigation, including the wrongful death lawsuits filed by victims’ families against the camp’s owners, the Eastland family.

The families and their attorneys did not hold back.

“The bankruptcy filing is not accountability. It is simply a financial reorganization that could allow the same people and entities to remain in control of Camp Mystic while attempting to circumvent the justice of the Court,” said Kyle Findley of Arnold & Itkin LLP. “After 27 girls died, this filing is just another attempt to delay taking responsibility.”

Findley represents six families in the wrongful death suit. Other attorneys echoed the sentiment.

“Bankruptcy will not stop all responsible parties from being held accountable,” said Paul Yetter, who represents seven victims’ families. “These innocent girls deserve justice.”

Sam Taylor of the Lanier Law Firm called the filing “another attempt to avoid accountability.”

A common misconception is that bankruptcy automatically wipes out lawsuits. It does not — it pauses them and reorganizes how claims are handled. Creditors, including families with wrongful death claims, can still pursue their cases within the bankruptcy process, though the path becomes more complex.

The Decision Not to Reopen

Pressure had been building for months before the bankruptcy filing. In April 2026, Camp Mystic withdrew its application to reopen for the summer, a day after an emotionally charged legislative hearing.

The camp had originally planned to welcome more than 800 girls to its Cypress Lake location in 2026. But families of victims and Texas lawmakers pushed hard against any reopening.

Texas Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick was among the most vocal. “In light of all this new information, the ongoing legislative investigation, your DSHS investigation and now the criminal investigation of Camp Mystic by the Texas Rangers, I reiterate, you should not renew or approve a camp license for Camp Mystic,” Patrick wrote.

So Camp Mystic will not reopen in 2026.

What This Means For You

If you are a parent considering a summer camp, the Camp Mystic tragedy underscores a hard truth: ask about emergency planning before you sign up. Specifically:

  1. Does the camp have a documented flood, fire, and severe-weather evacuation plan?
  2. How many trained staff are assigned to emergency response?
  3. Is the location in a known flood zone or near a river prone to flash flooding?
  4. What is the staff-to-camper ratio during an emergency, not just during activities?

These questions are no longer hypothetical. The investigation found that evacuating an entire camp rested on just three people — a structural failure that families say cost lives.

The Camp Mystic bankruptcy story is still unfolding. NBC’s Dateline aired a special, “After the Flood,” with Lester Holt investigating the tragedy, keeping national attention fixed on the question of accountability.

As the one-year anniversary of the July 4 floods approaches, the legal battle is far from over. The families have made one thing clear: they will not let a court filing be the final word.

If a preventable disaster takes the lives of children, where should the line be drawn between financial reorganization and moral responsibility? That is the question Texas — and the country — is now forced to confront.
