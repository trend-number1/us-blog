Zaccharie Risacher: The Surprising Fall of a No. 1 Pick

June 25, 2026 00:03 Trend Insights

How does the No. 1 overall pick in an NBA Draft go from starting almost every game to a DNP-CD in barely 18 months? That is the puzzle of Zaccharie Risacher, the French forward the Atlanta Hawks selected first overall in 2024 — and whose sophomore season became one of the most debated storylines in the league.

Quick Summary

  • Zaccharie Risacher was the No. 1 overall pick in the 2024 NBA Draft.
  • As a rookie he started 73 of 75 games and finished second in Rookie of the Year voting.
  • In his second season he was removed from the starting five in March and even logged a DNP-CD on April 8.
  • He called the season “frustrating” but insisted: “I belong on an NBA court.”

His story is a reminder that the path from prospect to cornerstone is rarely a straight line. Let’s break down what actually happened.

From Rookie Promise to Sophomore Struggle

When Atlanta drafted Risacher, the expectations were sky-high. As a rookie, he looked the part of a long-term building block.

He started 73 of 75 games in year one and finished runner-up for Rookie of the Year. For a teenager adjusting to the NBA, that was a strong foundation.

Year two told a different story. According to end-of-season numbers reported by ClutchPoints, Risacher averaged:

  • 9.6 points per game
  • 3.8 rebounds per game
  • 1.1 assists per game
  • Shooting splits of 45.5% from the field, 36.8% from three, and 64.4% from the free-throw line
  • 22.4 minutes across 67 games, with 46 starts

Those are respectable role-player numbers. But for a No. 1 overall pick projected as a future cornerstone, they registered as a step back. So what changed? The answer starts on the bench.

The Benching That Shook Atlanta

The turning point came in late February. After a quiet outing against the Miami Heat — zero points and two rebounds in 11 competitive minutes during a 128-97 loss — the Hawks made a decision.

In the very next game, a win over the Brooklyn Nets, veteran guard CJ McCollum entered the starting five and Risacher moved to the bench. The top pick of the 2024 draft was no longer a starter.

Coach Quin Snyder had hinted at his reasoning days earlier. Praising a rare 27-minute outing, Snyder pointed to effort: “He guarded, and he crashed the offensive boards… he played with a competitive fire that stood out.” The subtext was clear — that fire had to become the norm, not the exception.

The 50% insight: Risacher’s benching wasn’t just about one player. It coincided with a team-wide turnaround.

Here’s the surprising part. After he was removed from the starting lineup in March, the Hawks caught fire, closing the season on a 16-5 run and climbing from outside the playoff picture to a top-six seed. That context matters for the next chapter.

Why the Hawks’ Surge Made Things Worse for Risacher

A winning team is a hard thing to disrupt. As Atlanta surged, Risacher’s role shrank further.

Over the final 15 games, his minutes fell to just 16.3 per game, including that DNP-CD on April 8. By the playoffs, the squeeze became severe.

In Atlanta’s first playoff game, Risacher played a mere 2 minutes and 29 seconds. The Hawks’ first-round run ended in a lopsided 140-89 Game 6 loss to the New York Knicks.

The roster math worked against him, too. Atlanta is loaded at forward, especially after trading for Jonathan Kuminga, leaving fewer minutes for a still-developing young wing. The result was a season-long competition for a shrinking pool of opportunity.

The numbers paint a player caught between his draft pedigree and a roster that no longer prioritized his development. So how did Risacher himself handle it?

Risacher’s Response: Frustration Without Surrender

To his credit, Risacher has been candid rather than defensive. After Atlanta’s elimination, he reflected on a season defined by a shifting role.

“I feel like I just kept working,” he said. “I had to stay ready no matter what… fighting through adversity is part of the job.”

In June, he revisited the benchings more pointedly, calling the experience “frustrating” due to the lack of playing time — but adding the line that defined his offseason mindset: “I belong on an NBA court.”

The 75% insight: The flashes were real. In a March loss in Houston, Risacher scored 16 points off the bench. Earlier in the year against Phoenix, he posted 15 points, three rebounds, four assists, and two steals before a scary fall after a two-handed dunk left him with a hip contusion — in a game Atlanta won 124-122 after rallying from 20 down.

The talent that made him a No. 1 pick — length, shooting potential, and defensive versatility — hasn’t vanished. It’s been buried under circumstance. That distinction is everything when you ask what comes next.

What This Means For You

If you’re an NBA fan, a fantasy player, or just someone tracking the 2024 draft class, here are the practical takeaways.

  1. Don’t write off “draft busts” too early. Risacher is barely 20. Many top picks need two or three seasons to find footing, especially on crowded rosters.
  2. Context beats raw stats. A 9.6-point average looks underwhelming until you realize his team won by benching him. Role and fit can mask — or expose — talent.
  3. Watch the offseason, not the highlights. Reports have linked Risacher to a possible move, and several analysts argue a change of scenery could unlock him.

A common misconception worth correcting: being the No. 1 pick doesn’t guarantee superstardom. Risacher was widely viewed as a 3-and-D forward with ideal size, not a high-usage scorer. Judging him against unrealistic expectations is part of why the “bust” label spread so fast.

For Atlanta, the bold offseason question is whether to commit to developing him or trade him while his draft value still carries weight.

The Bottom Line

Zaccharie Risacher’s sophomore season was, by his own word, frustrating — a top pick reduced to spare minutes on a surging team. Yet the raw tools, the rookie-year promise, and his refusal to back down all suggest the story is far from over.

The real question isn’t whether Risacher can play in the NBA. It’s whether he gets the opportunity and fit to prove it — in Atlanta or somewhere new.

So here’s the question worth pondering: if a change of scenery is what Risacher needs, which team would you trust to bring out the player Atlanta drafted first overall?
