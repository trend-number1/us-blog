Retirement Age in America: The Surprising Truth Behind When Americans Actually Stop Working

April 9, 2026 00:03 Trend Insights

Americans are delaying retirement by four years on average as the cost of living continues to climb. Meanwhile, a growing number of workers are choosing to retire early rather than adapt to AI-driven workplaces. The question of when to retire has never been more complicated – or more consequential.

Key Takeaways:

  • The average American retires at 62, but full Social Security benefits don’t kick in until 67
  • Americans now say they need $1.46 million to retire comfortably
  • Rising costs are pushing expected retirement 4 years later than originally planned
  • Some workers are opting for early retirement rather than competing with AI

Retirement Age Is an “Ill-Defined Concept”

Ask five experts when Americans should retire, and you’ll get five different answers. That’s because, as Andrew Biggs, a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute, put it: “There isn’t a retirement age. It’s not that there’s a retirement age sitting out there and we haven’t discovered it. It’s just that it’s an ill-defined concept.”

The numbers tell a fragmented story. According to two respected annual surveys from the Employee Benefit Research Institute and the Transamerica Center for Retirement Studies, the average American actually retires at 62. But Boston College researchers, working from census data, place it slightly higher: 62.6 for women and 64.6 for men.

Here’s the timeline of key ages that shape retirement decisions:

AgeMilestone
62Earliest you can claim Social Security (reduced benefits)
65Medicare eligibility begins
67“Full” retirement age for Social Security
70Maximum Social Security benefits

For decades, 65 was viewed as the retirement age. Until the 1980s, it was the “normal” retirement age for Social Security, the age for Medicare, and the standard for most pensions. Then everything shifted.

In 1983, with Social Security facing insolvency, Congress passed legislation that gradually raised the full retirement age to 67. Pensions faded. The 401(k) system that replaced them rewarded Americans for working longer. And over recent decades, the average retirement age has crept steadily upward.

So what changed – and what does this mean for your plans?

The $1.46 Million Question

According to a recent CNBC report, the “magic number” Americans say they need to retire comfortably is $1.46 million. That figure represents a psychological benchmark – the amount people believe will sustain them through decades of post-work life.

For most Americans, that number feels impossibly far away. And the gap between aspiration and reality is widening.

A CBS News study found that Americans now expect to delay retirement by four years compared to their original plans, driven primarily by the rising cost of living. Housing, healthcare, and everyday expenses have all surged, forcing workers to recalculate their timelines.

How to assess where you stand:

  • Calculate your “retirement number” – multiply your expected annual expenses by 25 (the 4% rule)
  • Check your Social Security estimate at ssa.gov
  • Factor in healthcare costs between retirement and Medicare at 65
  • Consider part-time work as a bridge strategy
  • Maximize employer 401(k) matching – it’s free money you may be leaving on the table

The retirement savings crisis isn’t just about numbers. It’s about the psychological burden of uncertainty. As the Los Angeles Times editorial board noted, simply keeping seniors “out of poverty” during retirement isn’t enough. A dignified retirement requires planning that goes beyond survival.

But saving more isn’t the only trend reshaping retirement – technology is forcing a different kind of choice entirely.

AI Is Pushing Some Workers Into Early Retirement

Here’s an unexpected twist in the retirement story: while most Americans are delaying retirement, a subset of workers is choosing to retire early rather than adapt to AI.

The Wall Street Journal reported that some workers are opting to retire instead of taking on AI in their workplaces. Rather than learn new systems, retrain for AI-augmented roles, or compete with younger, more tech-fluent colleagues, these workers are walking away.

This creates a paradox. The same economy that demands people work longer is simultaneously making some jobs unrecognizable to veteran employees. For workers in their late 50s and early 60s, the calculus becomes: invest years in learning new technology, or take the retirement exit now?

Meanwhile, the policy landscape adds another layer of complexity. The Washington Post reported on concerns that recent labor policy proposals could function as a “massive 401(k) greed grab for Wall Street,” potentially affecting how retirement savings are managed and who benefits from them.

The intersection of AI disruption, policy shifts, and savings shortfalls is creating what might be the most challenging retirement planning environment in modern American history.

Smart Tax Planning Makes or Breaks Retirement

One often-overlooked aspect of retirement is withdrawal strategy. According to FedSmith, retirement success hinges on smart tax planning – and small mistakes can compound into significant losses over time.

Many retirees rely on “rules of thumb” that don’t account for their specific situations. The order in which you draw from taxable accounts, traditional IRAs, and Roth IRAs can make a six-figure difference over a 20- to 30-year retirement.

Common retirement tax mistakes to avoid:

  1. Taking Social Security too early without considering the tax implications
  2. Ignoring Roth conversions during low-income years between retirement and age 72
  3. Not accounting for Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) that can push you into higher tax brackets
  4. Overlooking state tax differences – some states don’t tax retirement income at all
  5. Failing to coordinate spousal Social Security claiming strategies

The difference between a well-planned withdrawal strategy and a haphazard one can mean the difference between running out of money at 82 and leaving a legacy at 92.

This brings us to the most important question: what should you actually do with all this information?

What This Means for You

Whether you’re 30 or 60, the retirement landscape of 2026 demands a more active approach than previous generations needed.

If you’re in your 20s-30s: Time is your greatest asset. Even modest 401(k) contributions now will compound dramatically. Don’t wait for the “perfect” amount – start with whatever you can.

If you’re in your 40s-50s: This is your catch-up window. Take advantage of catch-up contribution limits ($7,500 extra for 401(k) if you’re 50+). Run your Social Security projections now, not at 62.

If you’re nearing retirement: Focus on withdrawal sequencing and tax optimization. Consider whether a phased retirement – reducing hours rather than stopping entirely – could let your savings grow a few more critical years.

The $1.46 million target might seem daunting, but the real magic number is the one that covers your specific lifestyle in your specific location with your specific health needs.

The retirement age in America isn’t 62, 65, 67, or 70. It’s the age at which your preparation meets your reality. The only question that matters is: are you actively closing that gap, or hoping it closes itself?
