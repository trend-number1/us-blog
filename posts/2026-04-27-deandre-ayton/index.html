Deandre Ayton's Stunning Playoff Reinvention

April 27, 2026 12:03 Trend Insights

What if the most important player in the Los Angeles Lakers’ first-round sweep attempt isn’t Luka Doncic, Austin Reaves, or even LeBron James — but the center who openly bristled at being asked to play defense just months ago?

That’s the strange, fascinating story of Deandre Ayton right now. After a regular season where he averaged a quiet 12.5 points and 8 rebounds, Ayton has thrown himself into a defensive role he once resisted, and the Lakers have built a 3-0 lead over the Houston Rockets because of it.

Quick Summary

  • Lakers lead the Rockets 3-0 in the first round of the Western Conference playoffs
  • Ayton claims he can now “guard 1 through 5” after offseason and in-practice reps
  • His Game 1 line: 19 points, 11 rebounds, with Alperen Sengun held to 6-of-19 shooting
  • Production has dipped since (just 2 points in Game 3), but the Lakers won in overtime anyway
  • A potential second-round matchup with Oklahoma City’s big front line looms

Deandre Ayton’s Bold New Defensive Identity

Standing in front of reporters before Game 3 at Toyota Center, Ayton said something that would have sounded absurd six months ago.

“I’m about to show the world I can guard 1 through 5,” he told ESPN’s Dave McMenamin. “I thought it was going to be a secret weapon, but I’ve been practicing it and having multiple reps guarding the best players in the league on isolations. And the confidence is way up there.”

This is the same Deandre Ayton who, earlier in the season, publicly complained that the Lakers were trying to turn him into Clint Capela — a comment that drew a response from Capela himself.

So what changed? In short: the playoffs, and a coaching staff that wouldn’t let him drift.

The Sengun Assignment

The most visible proof has come in his matchup with Houston’s All-Star big man, Alperen Sengun. Sengun is one of the most skilled offensive centers in the league, and Ayton has spent the series matching his physicality, denying his catch points, and keeping him off the offensive glass.

In Game 1, Ayton held Sengun to 6-of-19 from the field while posting 19 points and 11 rebounds himself. It was the kind of two-way performance that makes a coaching staff exhale.

“It’s been fun,” Ayton said after a Thursday practice. “Especially just having a team out there that have your back and everybody just dialed in on the defensive end.”

That mood — a center enjoying defense — is the shift Lakers fans have been waiting two seasons to see.

Why Lakers Coach JJ Redick Keeps Repeating Himself

If you ask Lakers head coach JJ Redick about Ayton, you get the same answer almost word-for-word.

“The ceiling for our team is maximized when he’s playing at a high level,” Redick said the day after Game 1. “I remember saying that three times already in the last week. I don’t know how else to answer that.”

That repetition is the point. Redick is publicly anchoring the team’s ceiling to Ayton’s effort level — a not-so-subtle accountability tool used by coaches who believe a player has more in the tank.

The Inconsistency Problem

Here’s where the story gets complicated. Ayton’s production hasn’t been stable.

In the 112-108 overtime win in Game 3, he managed just two points on 1-of-3 shooting in 33 minutes. CBS Sports labeled it an “underwhelming” performance, and ESPN’s note on his game log was even sharper: “Ayton failed to have a meaningful impact for a second straight game.”

The Lakers won anyway. That speaks to LA’s depth — but it also raises the central question of this series.

What This Means for the Lakers’ Title Push

The Lakers are up 3-0 without Luka Doncic and Austin Reaves at full availability for parts of the series. That alone is impressive. But the next round won’t be this forgiving.

ESPN flagged the obvious concern: “A potential matchup with the Thunder is cause for concern given Oklahoma City’s sizable interior.”

Translation? Ayton’s “1 through 5” claim is about to get a real test.

Three Things to Watch

  1. Rebounding margin. Ayton’s offensive rebounding has quietly been a swing factor. When he posts double-digit boards, the Lakers’ second-chance points spike.
  2. Foul trouble vs. mobile bigs. Switching onto guards is glamorous; staying out of foul trouble while doing it is what actually wins playoff games.
  3. Scoring floor. The Lakers can survive a 2-point Ayton game against Houston. They probably can’t against Oklahoma City.

Common Misconceptions About Ayton’s Role

A lot of fan discussion gets this wrong. Let’s clear up two big ones.

Misconception #1: “Ayton just needs to score more.” Not exactly. The Lakers were actually more efficient in Game 3 — when he scored 2 — than in some games where he scored 20. With LeBron, Doncic, and Reaves on the roster, what LA needs from Ayton is rim protection, screen integrity, and rebounding, not 20-point nights.

Misconception #2: “He resented playing defense all season.” Ayton’s frustration was specifically with being told to play like Capela — a finishing, screen-and-roll specialist who doesn’t get post touches. The defensive effort is something he’s now embracing, even saying it’s “fun.”

That distinction matters. It’s the difference between a player who refused a role and a player who refused a specific framing of a role.

What This Means for You as a Fan

If you’re a Lakers fan, the read is simple: don’t anchor your hopes to Ayton’s box score. Anchor them to his defensive intensity. The 19-and-11 nights are nice, but the version of Ayton who locks in on Sengun for 30 minutes is the version that gets LA past Oklahoma City.

If you follow the league more broadly, Ayton’s arc is one of the most interesting case studies of the 2025-26 season: a former No. 1 overall pick, two teams later, finding a sustainable role only when the postseason stakes forced it.

The bigger question hangs over everything: is this who Deandre Ayton actually is now, or is it a two-week burst we’re going to over-index on?

The Rockets get one more chance to find out. Then, probably, so does Oklahoma City.

So here’s the thought to leave with — when a player says he can “guard 1 through 5,” do you believe the words, or do you wait for the second round to believe the tape?
