Mavericks vs Pelicans: Surprising Southwest Division Battle With Playoff Elimination Stakes

March 17, 2026 08:44 Trend Insights

The Dallas Mavericks have gone 4-19 over the last two months. The New Orleans Pelicans have gone 9-6 in their last 15 games. When these two Southwest Division rivals met at the Smoothie King Center on March 16, the contrast could not have been sharper — and neither could the stakes.

Key Takeaways

  • Dallas (23-45) entered on a 4-19 stretch over two months after officially embracing a tank strategy.
  • New Orleans (22-46) was riding its best stretch of basketball all season, going 9-6 in the last 15 games.
  • Cooper Flagg scored 27 points with 10 assists in Dallas’s shocking 130-120 upset of Cleveland the night before.
  • Dejounte Murray was listed as questionable due to illness after dropping a season-high 35 points against Houston.
  • Klay Thompson was downgraded to OUT, resting on the second night of a back-to-back.
  • The Pelicans led the season series 2-1 heading into this fourth and final meeting.

Mavericks vs Pelicans: A Tale of Two Trajectories

On paper, these teams look nearly identical. Dallas sat at 23-45, New Orleans at 22-46 — separated by just a single game near the bottom of the Western Conference. But the trajectories heading into Monday night told a completely different story.

The Pelicans were playing the best basketball of their season. A 9-6 record over their last 15 games had transformed a team that looked dead in the water into one of the more competitive squads in the league’s lower tier. A win on Monday would have given them the tiebreaker to overtake Dallas for the 12th seed in the West.

The Mavericks, meanwhile, had pulled the plug on their season. After an 8-game losing streak that cratered any remaining postseason hopes, Dallas officially pivoted to tank mode. They managed to win just 4 of 23 games over the previous two months — a staggering collapse for a franchise that reached the NBA Finals in 2024.

But here is the twist that made this game unpredictable: Dallas was coming off a 130-120 upset victory over the Cleveland Cavaliers — a team with a 41-27 record — just one night earlier. Could they carry that momentum into New Orleans on zero rest?

Cooper Flagg’s Star Turn Changes Everything

The headline from Dallas’s win over Cleveland was unmistakable. Cooper Flagg, the Mavericks’ prized rookie, put up 27 points and 10 assists against one of the best teams in the Eastern Conference. He led three Dallas players who scored 20 or more in the 130-120 road upset, and he did it as a 15-point underdog.

For a franchise that traded away its present to invest in Flagg’s future, performances like this are validation. The former Duke sensation is showing flashes of the generational talent that made him the most hyped prospect in years.

However, there was a catch. Flagg, along with PJ Washington and Naji Marshall, all played over 32 minutes in Cleveland. With less than 24 hours of rest before tip-off in New Orleans, fatigue was a genuine concern.

The Mavericks were essentially asking their young star to carry them on the second night of a back-to-back, with a depleted roster around him. It was a lot to ask — but it was also exactly the kind of development opportunity that tanking teams need.

Dejounte Murray’s Scoring Explosion and Illness Concern

On the Pelicans’ side, the spotlight belonged to Dejounte Murray, who was having arguably the best stretch of his career. Against the Houston Rockets on Friday, Murray poured in a season-high 35 points and nearly engineered a massive upset before New Orleans fell 107-105.

The Pelicans had a 4-point lead with 30 seconds remaining in that game before the Rockets snatched it away. Despite the heartbreaking finish, Murray’s performance signaled that he was locked in and playing at an elite level.

But there was a problem heading into Monday. Murray was listed as questionable due to illness on the official NBA injury report. Five Pelicans players total appeared on the injury report, creating uncertainty about New Orleans’ lineup.

Murray’s status was especially critical because of what the Pelicans look like without him. Here is the uncomfortable truth for New Orleans: their recent competitive surge has been driven largely by prioritizing veterans over young players. The team benched Derik Queen and Jeremiah Fears in recent weeks in favor of experienced players, which has helped them win more games but raised questions about long-term development.

  • The case for playing Murray: Pelicans don’t control their 2026 draft pick, reducing the incentive to tank
  • The case for resting Murray: An illness on a non-critical game is not worth the risk
  • The development dilemma: Wins are coming at the potential expense of young player minutes

The Season Series and Why Home Court Mattered

Heading into this fourth and final meeting of the season, the Pelicans led the series 2-1. The history of these matchups told a story of tight, competitive games.

The most recent meeting came on December 22 in New Orleans, where the Pelicans won 119-113 as 1-point underdogs. Dallas had built an 8-point lead entering the fourth quarter before watching it evaporate. In November, the teams split a pair at the Smoothie King Center — New Orleans winning 101-99 on November 5 and Dallas taking the next one 118-115 on November 21.

Every game had been decided by six points or fewer. This rivalry, even between two struggling teams, produced genuine drama.

Home court was a factor worth monitoring. The Mavericks entered with a dismal 9-24 road record, making them one of the worst traveling teams in the NBA. The Pelicans, meanwhile, were well-rested after last playing on Friday, giving them a significant energy advantage over a Dallas squad playing its second game in two nights.

Klay Thompson Shut Down Again as Dallas Protects Veterans

One of the most telling storylines of the Mavericks’ tank was the decision to downgrade Klay Thompson to OUT on Monday. The veteran sharpshooter was not dealing with an injury — he was simply resting on the second night of a back-to-back.

This has become standard practice for Dallas. Since acquiring Thompson, the organization has been deliberate about managing his workload. The four-time All-Star missed two full seasons during his time with the Golden State Warriors due to a torn ACL and Achilles tendon. Those career-altering injuries have made load management a permanent part of his routine.

With Kyrie Irving and Dereck Lively already shut down for the season, and Daniel Gafford listed as doubtful, the Mavericks were rolling out what amounted to a development squad with Cooper Flagg as the centerpiece.

This is the reality of the 2025-26 Mavericks: a team that went to the NBA Finals just two years ago and is now actively sitting healthy players to preserve draft positioning. The transformation has been swift and, for Dallas fans, jarring.

What This Means for Both Franchises

The Mavericks vs Pelicans matchup is more than just a late-season game between two struggling teams. It is a window into two different rebuilding philosophies.

Dallas has committed to the tank. They have shut down veterans, embraced losses, and are betting everything on the draft lottery. Cooper Flagg’s development is the silver lining, but the franchise is clearly playing for 2027 and beyond.

New Orleans is taking the opposite approach, pushing for wins despite a losing record. With no control over their 2026 draft pick, the Pelicans have less incentive to lose — but the decision to bench young players like Queen and Fears could have consequences. Are short-term wins worth sacrificing long-term growth?

As the final 15 games of the regular season play out, both organizations face decisions that will echo for years. The Mavericks need to determine whether their young core — led by Flagg — is ready to compete as soon as next season. The Pelicans must figure out whether their veteran-heavy approach is building a winning culture or simply masking deeper roster issues.

One thing is clear: in a game between two teams at the bottom of the West, the real competition was not about who wins the fourth quarter. It was about who wins the next five years.
