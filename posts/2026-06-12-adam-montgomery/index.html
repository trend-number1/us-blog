Adam Montgomery Conviction Overturned: Shocking NH Ruling

June 12, 2026 00:03 Trend Insights

A father convicted of killing his 5-year-old daughter just had that murder conviction wiped away — and he is still going to prison. How does that happen?

On June 11, 2026, the New Hampshire Supreme Court reversed Adam Montgomery’s second-degree murder conviction in the 2019 death of his daughter, Harmony Montgomery. The decision did not declare him innocent. It did not free him. But it did expose a critical flaw in how the original trial was conducted, sending one of New England’s most disturbing child homicide cases back into the courts.

Key Takeaways

  • The NH Supreme Court reversed Adam Montgomery’s second-degree murder conviction on June 11, 2026.
  • Convictions for second-degree assault, falsifying physical evidence, witness tampering, and abuse of a corpse were affirmed.
  • The court ruled the assault and murder charges should not have been tried together.
  • Montgomery remains incarcerated on the affirmed convictions.
  • A separate $15.5 million wrongful death judgment was issued against him in May 2026.

What the Adam Montgomery Ruling Actually Says

The ruling is narrower than headlines suggest. Justices did not say Adam Montgomery was wrongly accused or that the evidence was weak. They said the trial itself was structured in a way that violated his right to a fair proceeding on the homicide charge specifically.

At the core: the assault charge and the murder charge were tried together. Montgomery’s appellate lawyer argued during the October 2025 hearing that these should have been separated. The state pushed back, claiming that “any error with respect to joinder was also harmless as to the second degree murder charge because the state’s case and the evidence of the defendant’s guilt were overwhelming.”

The justices disagreed. In their written opinion, they concluded: “the misjoinder of offenses was not harmless as to the homicide charge. Accordingly, we reverse the defendant’s conviction of second-degree murder.”

In plain language: combining the charges may have prejudiced the jury when it came time to decide the most serious one.

That distinction matters more than most readers realize — and it leads directly to the next question.

Why He Is Still Behind Bars

This is the part that confuses people. If the murder conviction is gone, why isn’t Montgomery walking free?

Because four other convictions were affirmed:

  1. Second-degree assault — tied to the documented abuse of Harmony
  2. Falsifying physical evidence
  3. Witness tampering
  4. Abuse of a corpse

Montgomery was originally sentenced in 2024 to 56 years to life after a jury found him guilty on all charges. Prosecutors had presented evidence that Harmony suffered repeated beatings — including punches to the head — before her death in 2019, and that Montgomery concealed and disposed of her body for months while misleading others about her whereabouts. Her body has never been recovered.

Those affirmed convictions carry substantial prison time on their own. The murder reversal does not erase them.

So the case is not over. It has been sent back to a lower court for further proceedings on the homicide charge. Prosecutors can attempt to retry that count separately.

This sets up the next legal battle — and a very different financial one is already underway.

The $15.5 Million Wrongful Death Judgment

Here is the insight most coverage glosses over: criminal and civil cases run on entirely different tracks, with different standards of proof.

In May 2026 — about a month before the criminal reversal — a $15.5 million judgment was issued against Adam Montgomery in a wrongful death lawsuit. According to Court TV’s reporting, that civil case had been reinstated by a judge after earlier procedural challenges.

Civil verdicts require only a preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not), while criminal convictions demand proof beyond a reasonable doubt. That gap is why a defendant can be held civilly liable for a death even when a criminal conviction is later overturned.

What this means practically: the appellate ruling on the murder charge does not undo the civil judgment. The $15.5 million remains.

It also highlights a misconception worth correcting before moving on.

Common Misconceptions About “Overturned” Cases

Headlines using the word “overturned” routinely trigger the same misunderstandings. Let’s address the three biggest ones.

  • Misconception 1: “Overturned” means innocent. It does not. Appellate courts rule on legal process, not factual guilt. As reporting from WMUR, NBC Boston, and the Boston Globe consistently notes, the New Hampshire Supreme Court made no finding about whether Montgomery caused Harmony’s death.
  • Misconception 2: He gets released immediately. No. Multiple affirmed convictions keep him incarcerated.
  • Misconception 3: The case is closed. The opposite. The case returns to the lower court, where prosecutors can pursue the murder charge again — this time presumably without joining it to the assault count.

Understanding these distinctions changes how you read every “conviction overturned” headline going forward.

What This Means For You

Most readers will never sit on a jury for a homicide trial. But the Adam Montgomery ruling is a real-time lesson in how the American legal system handles procedural fairness vs. substantive guilt — and why the two are deliberately separated.

A few practical takeaways:

  • Trial structure matters as much as evidence. Prosecutors won a conviction with what they called “overwhelming” evidence. The state’s highest court still reversed it because of how charges were joined. Process is not a technicality — it is the system’s check against jury prejudice.
  • Civil and criminal outcomes can diverge. A $15.5 million wrongful death judgment can coexist with a reversed murder conviction. If you ever follow a high-profile case, watch both tracks.
  • “Overturned” ≠ “exonerated.” Train yourself to read past the headline before forming an opinion.

For families touched by child welfare cases — Harmony Montgomery had been the subject of prior court involvement before her 2019 death — the ruling is also a reminder that legal accountability rarely arrives in a single, clean verdict. It comes in layers, and sometimes those layers contradict each other.

So here is the question worth sitting with: If a court can reverse a conviction without declaring innocence, what does “justice” actually look like for a victim like Harmony Montgomery — and who decides?

The case now returns to a New Hampshire trial court. Whatever happens next, the affirmed convictions ensure that Adam Montgomery’s story is far from over.
