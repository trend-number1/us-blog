RKLB Stock: The Surprising Truth Behind a 49% Plunge

July 17, 2026 00:03 Trend Insights

Here’s a fact that stops most investors cold: RKLB stock rocketed more than 600% higher in a single year — then gave back nearly half of it in a matter of weeks.

So which story is real? The unstoppable SpaceX challenger, or an overpriced rocket maker crashing back to Earth?

The truth, as usual, lives somewhere in the middle. And the numbers behind Rocket Lab tell a far more interesting tale than the headlines suggest.

Quick Summary

  • RKLB stock is down roughly 49% from its recent highs, sliding about 27% in just the past month.
  • 2025 revenue hit $601.8 million, up 37.96% year-over-year — but the company still lost $198.2 million.
  • 16 analysts rate the stock a “Buy” with a 12-month price target of $116.57, implying nearly 69% upside.
  • The bull case hinges on Neutron, Rocket Lab’s heavy-lift rocket built to compete with SpaceX.

Why RKLB Stock Is Sliding Right Now

Let’s start with the pain. RKLB stock has fallen about 27% over the past month, part of a broader selloff that has dragged shares down roughly 49% from their peak.

The trigger wasn’t a failed launch or a lost contract. It was something more mundane: valuation.

At its recent highs, Rocket Lab was valued at nearly $22 billion — a hefty price tag for a company generating around $500 million in annual sales and still operating deep in the red. When investors reassessed that math, gravity took over.

Wall Street noticed the divide, too. Piper Sandler analyst Alexander Potter initiated coverage with a cautious Neutral rating and an $83 price target, openly preferring rival AST SpaceMobile (ASTS) for its “more palatable valuation.”

But a falling price and a shaky story aren’t the same thing. To see why, you have to look at what Rocket Lab actually builds.

What Rocket Lab Actually Does

Rocket Lab Corporation is far more than a launch company with a catchy name. It operates across two distinct segments: launch services and space systems.

The company designs and manufactures small and medium-class rockets, builds spacecraft and components, and provides optical systems, on-orbit management, and constellation management services. Its reach spans the United States, Canada, Japan, and international markets.

That diversification matters. Here’s how the business breaks down:

  • Launch services — small and medium-class rocket launches
  • Spacecraft design & manufacturing — building the satellites themselves
  • Spacecraft components — selling parts to other space companies
  • On-orbit & constellation management — ongoing revenue after launch

This is the part most casual investors miss: Rocket Lab isn’t just selling rides to orbit. It’s selling the hardware, the design work, and the long-term management that keeps satellites running.

And that brings us to the single biggest catalyst in the entire RKLB story.

The Neutron Bet: Rocket Lab vs. SpaceX

Here’s the insight that separates the bulls from the skeptics: everything hinges on Neutron.

Neutron is Rocket Lab’s heavy-lift rocket, engineered to carry major payloads and compete directly with SpaceX for the biggest, most lucrative launch contracts. In a recent milestone, the company completed its Neutron launch pad, giving investors fresh confidence that a successful launch is within reach.

Rocket Lab’s CEO framed the rivalry bluntly, calling SpaceX a “friendly rival” and noting that Rocket Lab has outlasted more than 100 competitors in the brutal space-launch business.

That survival isn’t luck. It’s a moat. While dozens of would-be rocket companies burned through cash and folded, Rocket Lab kept flying — and kept booking a substantial backlog of contracts.

Still, momentum isn’t the same as profit. So what do the raw financials really say?

The Financial Reality Check

Let’s put the numbers side by side, because this is where the debate gets sharp.

The revenue growth is genuinely impressive — nearly 38% year-over-year. But notice the second row: losses actually widened by 4.22%.

Building rockets is capital-intensive. Depreciation costs are heavy, and the company has warned it will need to fund continued development through expensive debt or stock dilution. That’s the uncomfortable trade-off behind the growth.

The common misconception? That a “Buy” rating and a $116.57 target mean the stock is a sure thing. It doesn’t. Those targets assume Neutron succeeds and margins improve. If either falters, the valuation has room to fall further.

What This Means for You

So how should a real investor read all this?

First, understand what you’re buying. RKLB stock is not a value play — it’s a growth bet on the commercial space economy, priced for a future that hasn’t fully arrived yet.

Here’s some actionable framing:

  1. Match it to your risk tolerance. A stock that can swing 600% up and 49% down in a year is not a “set it and forget it” holding.
  2. Watch Neutron, not the daily ticker. A successful heavy-lift launch is the milestone that could re-rate the entire company. Short-term dips around the 200-day moving average are noise by comparison.
  3. Respect the dilution risk. More shares issued to fund growth means your slice of the pie can shrink even if the company thrives.
  4. Compare within the sector. Analysts like Piper Sandler prefer ASTS on valuation — a reminder that RKLB isn’t the only ticket to space exposure.

The bigger picture is what makes this compelling. A company that survived more than 100 competitors, grew revenue 38%, and built a launch pad for a SpaceX-class rocket is not a company to dismiss because of one rough month.

But it’s also not cheap, and it’s still losing money.

So here’s the question worth sitting with: Are you buying RKLB stock because you believe in the space economy of 2030 — or because a 49% discount simply feels like a bargain? Those are two very different bets, and only one of them survives a Neutron delay.

Do your own research, size your position for the volatility, and never confuse a falling price with a discount.
