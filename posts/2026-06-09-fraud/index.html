Fraud Losses Hit Record $15.9 Billion: The Surprising 2026 Reality

June 9, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

Americans lost a staggering $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025 — a record-shattering figure that exposes just how aggressive, organized, and digitally savvy today’s scammers have become. If you think fraud is somebody else’s problem, the latest numbers from the Federal Trade Commission suggest otherwise.

Key Takeaways

  • Consumer fraud losses hit a record $15.9 billion in 2025, up from $12.5 billion in 2024 (FTC).
  • Investment scams alone accounted for nearly half — $7.9 billion.
  • Imposter scams generated more than 1 million reports and $3.5 billion in losses.
  • The SBA referred 562,000 suspected fraudulent pandemic loans worth $22.2 billion to Treasury for collection in April 2026.
  • Reported fraud losses have surged nearly 430% since 2020.

The scope of fraud in America has shifted dramatically in just five years. What used to be isolated cons targeting the elderly has become a multibillion-dollar industry blending text messages, social media, and impersonation tactics that fool people of every age group. Below, we unpack the numbers — and what they actually mean for your wallet.

The $15.9 Billion Wake-Up Call

The Federal Trade Commission’s 2025 data, presented by Lois Greisman, associate director of the FTC’s division of marketing practices, in testimony before the U.S. Congressional Joint Economic Committee on March 25, paints a stark picture. Fraud losses jumped from $12.5 billion in 2024 to $15.9 billion in 2025 — a year-over-year increase of roughly 27%.

Equally alarming is the volume. The FTC received 3 million fraud reports from consumers in 2025, up from 2.6 million the year before. That’s nearly 8,200 new fraud reports every single day.

And remember: these are only the reports that get filed. The actual losses are widely believed to be significantly higher, since many victims never come forward out of embarrassment or because they don’t realize they were defrauded.

Why the explosive growth? Greisman noted that reported fraud losses have risen nearly 430% since 2020 — a trajectory that suggests fraud is now scaling with technology rather than being suppressed by it.

That brings us to where the money is actually disappearing.

Where the Money Goes: Investment Scams Dominate

The single largest fraud category in 2025 wasn’t lottery scams or fake IRS calls. It was investment fraud, which accounted for $7.9 billion — nearly half of all reported losses.

Investment scams typically promise outsized returns from cryptocurrency, foreign exchange trades, or “guaranteed” stock picks. Victims are often drawn in through social media ads or direct messages, then walked through fake trading platforms designed to display imaginary gains.

Here’s a quick comparison of how the major scam categories stacked up in 2025:

Scam TypeReported Losses (2025)Key Channel
Investment scams~$7.9 billionSocial media, messaging apps
Imposter scams$3.5+ billionPhone, text
Social media-initiated fraud (all types)$2+ billionSocial media platforms

Social media was the top contact method by reported losses, with consumers reporting aggregate losses of more than $2 billion in fiscal year 2025 tied to social media-initiated fraud.

If investment scams represent the biggest dollar haul, the most common attack vector tells a different story — and it’s even more personal.

Imposter Scams: The New Face of Fraud

The most frequently reported fraud category in 2025 was imposter scams — schemes where someone pretends to be a person or authority the victim trusts.

According to the FTC, more than 1 million imposter scam reports rolled in last year, with over $3.5 billion in losses. Scammers impersonated:

  • The FBI, FTC, and IRS
  • Banks and credit unions
  • Grandchildren in distress
  • Local sheriffs
  • Online romantic interests

Text messaging was the top contact method reported by consumers when identifying how scammers first reached them. A simple “Hi Mom” or “This is your bank’s fraud department” text can be the opening line of a multi-thousand-dollar loss.

A common misconception

Many people still believe fraud primarily targets older adults. The data suggests otherwise — younger consumers report fraud at higher rates, although older adults tend to lose more money per incident. Nobody is too tech-savvy to be scammed. Modern fraud campaigns are designed by professional operators using AI-generated voices, cloned websites, and personalized scripts pulled from leaked data.

That’s the retail side of fraud. But the institutional side may be even more staggering.

The Institutional Front: Pandemic Loans and State Programs

While consumers lost billions to scams, government agencies are still cleaning up an even larger mess from pandemic-era programs.

On April 24, 2026, the U.S. Small Business Administration announced its largest fraud referral on record: 562,000 suspected fraudulent loans, tied to $22.2 billion in delinquent Paycheck Protection Program (PPP) and COVID Economic Injury Disaster (EIDL) loans, were sent to the U.S. Department of Treasury for collection. The borrowers were also transmitted to the Department of Justice.

According to the SBA, these loans had been previously flagged for suspected fraud during the Biden Administration but were never sent to Treasury for collection or referred to DOJ for investigation. SBA Administrator Kelly Loeffler said the agency was acting “with the strong support of the White House Task Force to Eliminate Fraud” to recoup stolen pandemic-era funds.

At the state level, a 205-page final staff report released by the House Oversight Committee on June 8, 2026 alleges that senior Minnesota officials, including Governor Tim Walz, failed for years to act on warnings about fraud in state social services programs. The report concludes:

“Fraud warnings were elevated to the most senior levels of the Minnesota state government, meaningful corrective action was delayed or avoided, and payments continued long after credible signs of fraud emerged.”

Congressional investigators reportedly spoke with nearly 30 whistleblowers, some of whom accused the Walz administration of retaliation for sounding the alarm. The report alleges that hundreds of millions of dollars in confirmed or alleged losses occurred, with billions more at risk.

The takeaway? Fraud isn’t just an individual problem — it’s a structural one, and it’s happening at every level of the economy.

What This Means for You

So how do you protect yourself when fraud is growing nearly 430% faster than it was five years ago? Practical steps you can take today:

  1. Treat unsolicited texts and DMs as suspicious by default. If your “bank” texts you, call the number on the back of your card instead of clicking any link.
  2. Verify before you wire. No legitimate federal agency — IRS, FBI, FTC — will demand payment by gift card, crypto, or wire transfer.
  3. Be skeptical of “guaranteed” investment returns. If it sounds too good to be true on Instagram or TikTok, it almost certainly is.
  4. Set up transaction alerts on every bank, brokerage, and credit card account. Speed of detection matters.
  5. Report suspected fraud at reportfraud.ftc.gov. Reporting feeds the data that enables enforcement.

The bottom line

Fraud in 2026 isn’t a back-alley operation — it’s an industry. With $15.9 billion lost in a single year and consumer reports rising sharply, every American should assume they will be targeted at some point. The question isn’t if a scam will reach your inbox, but how prepared you’ll be when it does.

What’s the last suspicious message you ignored? Sharing that story with a friend or family member could be the simplest, cheapest fraud-prevention tool available — and it costs nothing.
