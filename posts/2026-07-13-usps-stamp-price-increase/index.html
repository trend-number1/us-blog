USPS Stamp Price Increase: What the 82-Cent Forever Stamp Means

July 13, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

Here’s a number that might surprise you: the USPS stamp price increase that took effect on July 12, 2026, means a Forever stamp now costs exactly double what it did when it debuted 19 years ago.

The U.S. Postal Service raised the price of a First-Class Mail Forever stamp from 78 cents to 82 cents this past Sunday. It’s a modest four-cent bump on paper, but it caps off one of the most relentless stretches of postage hikes in the agency’s history.

Quick Summary

  • Forever stamps rose from 78¢ to 82¢ on July 12, 2026
  • Overall mailing services prices climbed roughly 4.8%
  • This is the sixth increase in five years
  • Stamps you already own never expire — no need to add postage
  • The increase was driven by billions in USPS losses in FY2024 and FY2025

So why does mailing a simple letter keep getting more expensive? Let’s break down exactly what changed.

What Changed With the USPS Stamp Price Increase

The headline is the Forever stamp, but the USPS stamp price increase touched several categories of mail at once. Across all mailing services products, the Postal Service raised prices about 4.8%.

Here are the key changes that took effect July 12, 2026:

  • Forever stamp (First-Class letter): 78¢ → 82¢ (+4¢)
  • Domestic postcard: 61¢ → 65¢ (+4¢)
  • Metered 1-ounce letter: 74¢ → 78¢ (+4¢)
  • International postcard and 1-ounce letter: → $1.75 (+5¢)
  • Additional ounce (single-piece letters): 29¢ — unchanged

The metered rate matters if you use a postage meter at home or work — it stays four cents cheaper than the retail Forever stamp, a small nudge toward the more efficient option.

One thing that did not change: the extra-ounce charge for heavier letters held steady at 29 cents. If you regularly mail thicker envelopes, that’s a small consolation.

But the bigger question isn’t what changed — it’s how the Postal Service got here.

Why Stamp Prices Keep Climbing

The Postal Service didn’t hide the reason. In its April filing, the agency pointed squarely at “the severe financial crisis facing the Postal Service and continued rising operational costs.”

The USPS posted billions of dollars in losses in both fiscal year 2024 and fiscal year 2025. A Postal Regulatory Commission financial review published in May found that in FY2025, USPS expenses outpaced revenue — a gap the agency is trying to close through pricing authority granted under its Delivering for America plan.

Here’s the insight most people miss: the Postal Service generally receives no tax dollars for operating expenses. It funds itself almost entirely through the sale of postage, products, and services. When mail volume shrinks year after year, raising prices is one of the few levers it has.

Since 2022, USPS has moved to a regular price-adjustment schedule, typically raising rates twice a year in January and July. That cadence explains why increases now feel almost routine.

The regulator didn’t rubber-stamp the change without concern, either. After USPS filed its proposal on April 9, 2026, the Postal Regulatory Commission approved it on May 27 — but flagged worries about USPS delivery performance, shrinking letter volumes, and the overall state of its finances.

So how does 82 cents compare to where we started? The long view is striking.

How 82 Cents Compares to History

When Forever stamps launched in 2007, they cost 41 cents each. Reaching 82 cents means the price has doubled — a 100% increase in 19 years.

Zoom in on just the recent stretch and the acceleration is obvious. The price has climbed from 58 cents in 2021 to 82 cents in 2026, a roughly 41% jump in five years.

That’s six increases in five years — a pace that has reshaped what Americans expect from the mailbox. USPS held stamp prices steady at the start of 2026, but it still raised shipping rates in January for Priority Mail, Priority Mail Express, USPS Ground Advantage, and Parcel Select.

Even so, the Postal Service argues its rates “remain among the most affordable in the world.” Whether that comfort lands depends on how much mail you actually send.

Which brings us to the practical question: what should you do about it?

What This Means For You

First, a common misconception worth clearing up: your existing stamps are still good. A Forever stamp is a First-Class stamp with no price printed on it, and it’s always worth the current one-ounce letter rate — no matter when you bought it.

That means a Forever stamp purchased for 41 cents in 2007, or 78 cents last month, still mails a one-ounce letter today with nothing to add. As the agency puts it, “The Forever Stamp always represents the current price of a one (1) ounce First-Class Mail postage.”

Here’s the actionable takeaway: buy before the next increase to lock in the lower price. Because USPS follows a twice-yearly schedule, the strategy is simple — stock up on Forever stamps whenever prices are steady, and you effectively freeze your postage cost.

A few practical moves:

  1. Stock up when prices are flat — the next adjustment could land as early as January 2027.
  2. Check USPS Notice 123 — the official, authoritative price sheet — before assuming any rate.
  3. Consider metered postage if you mail frequently; it runs four cents cheaper per letter.
  4. Go digital where you can — every bill paid online is a stamp you’ll never buy.

And if you’re a collector, there’s a bright side: USPS paired this increase with new stamp releases, including a Barbie series.

The four-cent hike won’t break anyone’s budget on a single letter. But it’s a reminder of a slow, steady shift — the humble mailbox is quietly becoming a premium service.

So here’s the real question to sit with: as stamp prices double every two decades and mail volume keeps falling, how much longer will the letter in your mailbox stay affordable at all?
