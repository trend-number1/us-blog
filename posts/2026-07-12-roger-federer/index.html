Roger Federer Returns: Surprising 2026 US Open Comeback

What does a man with 20 Grand Slam titles do three years after saying goodbye? If you are Roger Federer, you quietly pick up the racket again.

The Swiss legend has been confirmed to play the 2026 US Open, marking his return to Flushing Meadows seven years after his last appearance there in 2019. For millions of fans, it is the news they never expected to hear again.

Key Takeaways

  • Roger Federer will play the 2026 US Open, his first appearance there since 2019.
  • He’ll feature in a doubles match on Tuesday, August 25th, alongside Andy Roddick, John McEnroe, and Andre Agassi.
  • Federer, now 44, retired in 2022 but is playing tennis two to three times a week again.
  • He won 20 Grand Slam titles and 103 singles titles across a 24-year career.

Let’s break down exactly what is happening, why it matters, and what this comeback means for you as a fan.

Roger Federer’s US Open Comeback: The Details

The 20-time Grand Slam champion retired in 2022 following an emotional appearance at the Laver Cup. Now, he is slowly returning to the sport he dominated for so long.

Federer will play a doubles match during the qualifying stages of the tournament. The match is scheduled for Tuesday, August 25th at Flushing Meadows.

He won’t be alone. His partners for the evening read like a tennis hall of fame:

  • Andy Roddick — former world number one and 2003 US Open champion
  • John McEnroe — seven-time Grand Slam singles champion
  • Andre Agassi — eight-time major winner

In a statement to the Grand Slam, Federer explained why New York holds a special place in his heart. “The US Open has always been one of the most special tournaments for me,” he said. “So many unforgettable moments of my career happened in New York, and Arthur Ashe Stadium is a place that means a great deal to me.”

He added: “I’ve missed being part of that atmosphere and feeling the incredible energy that the fans bring every year.”

But how did a retired 44-year-old find his way back onto the court? The answer starts months before New York.

From Retirement to a Slow Return

Federer’s comeback did not happen overnight. It has been a gradual reintroduction across the 2026 season.

He first returned to the Australian Open with an exhibition match. The event was part of the tournament’s inaugural Open Ceremony, featuring fellow former world number ones alongside him.

Announcing that appearance, Federer reflected warmly on the tournament he once nicknamed the “Happy Slam.” He said it “feels like a lifetime ago” that he coined the phrase, adding that it “still makes me smile when I think about all the moments I’ve had here.”

He also played a practice session with Casper Ruud, one of today’s top professionals — a sign that this is more than just a ceremonial wave to the crowd.

Here is a quick look at how Federer’s 2026 return has unfolded:

The most surprising part? Federer says his body finally feels good again. And that leads to the story of why he had to walk away in the first place.

The Injury Battle Behind His Retirement

Federer was famous for one thing besides his elegant game: he almost never got injured. But behind the scenes, the end of his career was far more painful than fans realized.

Appearing on the Served podcast with old rival Andy Roddick, Federer opened up like never before. He revealed he was using crutches to get around when his knee problem became chronic a year before retirement.

Roddick recalled seeing him in Boston in 2021. “You were hiding your crutches to take photos,” he said. “Your knee was terrible.” Federer laughed, replying, “Those crutches do not need to be in the pictures!”

This is a common misconception worth correcting: many assumed Federer retired simply because of age. In reality, chronic knee problems — requiring surgery and long recovery — forced the decision.

“I was always famous for never being injured,” Federer said. “I dealt with some back issues, hand stuff, foot things, but never to the extent like it was with the knee at the end.”

Notably, Federer never once retired mid-match across a career spanning 1,750 matches in singles and doubles. That streak of durability makes his hidden struggle all the more remarkable.

So how is he feeling now, three years later? The answer explains everything about this comeback.

How Often Is Federer Playing Now?

Retirement hasn’t kept Federer off the court entirely. He recently confirmed just how much tennis he is playing again.

Speaking to the Tennis Channel, the 103-time ATP champion said he is now playing “like two or three times a week.” He credited a long break for helping his knee recover. “I played a ton of golf,” he noted. “That was also the time to give my knee a good rest.”

Even in retirement, one part of his game never fully switched off. “I’ve always served every time I go on court with my children,” he said, because “that thing gets really rusty quickly.”

His verdict on his own body is telling: “I’m really happy three years later that my body is still as good as it is.”

Federer also remains deeply connected to the sport. He made four appearances at the Laver Cup, claiming 18 points from his 12 matches, and continues to attend as a spectator and mentor to younger players.

What This Means for You

If you are a tennis fan, this is your chance to see one of the greatest players of all time back on the biggest stage. But manage your expectations — this is an exhibition and doubles appearance, not a competitive singles run.

Here is why it still matters:

  1. A rare live moment — Four legends on one court is something you may never see again.
  2. A nostalgia celebration — This is about honoring memories at Arthur Ashe Stadium, not chasing titles.
  3. Proof that legends age gracefully — Federer at 44, playing pain-free, is an inspiring reminder that passion doesn’t retire.

For context, Federer sits behind Novak Djokovic (24 majors) and Rafael Nadal (22 majors) on the all-time Grand Slam list with his 20 titles. He spent a total of 310 weeks as world number one, a testament to his sustained dominance.

The Bottom Line

Roger Federer’s return to the 2026 US Open is not a bid for glory — it’s a celebration. After a career of 20 Grand Slams, 103 singles titles, and 24 years as a professional, he is choosing to reconnect with the fans and the arena that shaped him.

The evening of August 25th promises nostalgia, star power, and one more chance to watch a master at work.

So here is the question worth pondering: when a champion has already won everything, what keeps pulling them back to the court? Maybe the answer is the simplest one of all — the love of the game never really retires.
