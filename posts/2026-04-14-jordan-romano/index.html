Jordan Romano’s Stunning Comeback: From Phillies Castoff to Angels’ Perfect Closer

What does perfection look like in Major League Baseball? For Jordan Romano, it looks like six appearances, zero hits allowed, and a flawless 0.00 ERA to open the 2026 season with the Los Angeles Angels.

Just months ago, this two-time All-Star was considered damaged goods — a reliever whose best days seemed behind him. Now he’s making every team that passed on him look foolish.

Key Takeaways:

  • Romano has a 0.00 ERA with 4 saves in 6 appearances and zero hits allowed for the Angels in 2026
  • He signed a bargain 1-year, $2 million deal after being let go by the Phillies
  • His 7 strikeouts and 0.40 WHIP rank him among the top pitchers in baseball early this season
  • Advanced metrics from 2024-2025 suggested he was pitching better than his inflated ERA indicated

From All-Star to Unwanted: Jordan Romano’s Rocky Two-Year Slide

It’s hard to overstate how dominant Romano was during his peak years with the Toronto Blue Jays. Across the 2022 and 2023 seasons, he logged 36 saves each year with a sub-3.00 ERA, earning back-to-back All-Star selections and cementing himself as one of the game’s elite closers. Over eight major-league seasons, he accumulated 117 career saves with a 3.67 career ERA and 1.18 WHIP.

Then things fell apart — fast.

In 2024, Romano battled persistent elbow inflammation in his right throwing arm that limited him to just 13.2 innings across 15 games. He started the year on the 15-day injured list, was briefly activated in mid-April, and then returned to the IL for the remainder of the season. The Blue Jays, after six seasons with their Canadian-born closer, chose not to tender him a contract, making him a free agent for the first time in his career.

The Philadelphia Phillies took a chance, signing Romano to an $8.5 million deal heading into 2025. But the gamble didn’t pay off. He posted a brutal 12.60 ERA through his first six appearances as a Phillie, surrendering six hits and seven earned runs. He lost his closer role before the All-Star break and finished the season with just eight total saves. Philadelphia opted not to bring him back.

At 32 years old, the Markham, Ontario native was running out of opportunities. But the numbers told a more nuanced story than the headlines suggested.

The Hidden Story in Romano’s Advanced Metrics

Here’s what most casual observers missed during Romano’s struggles: his expected stats told a very different story from his actual results.

The gap between Romano’s 8.23 ERA and 4.08 xERA in 2025 is staggering. Expected ERA (xERA) strips away the noise of sequencing and luck, focusing on the quality of contact a pitcher allows. That spread suggested Romano was getting historically unlucky — his pitches were better than the scoreboard indicated.

Both his four-seam fastball and slider had lost approximately 1 mph of velocity, which is notable but not career-ending for a pitcher with his repertoire and command. Smart evaluators saw a pitcher whose underlying stuff was still viable, even if the results hadn’t caught up yet.

The Angels were paying attention — and they acted decisively.

A Bargain Deal That’s Paying Off Immediately

Los Angeles signed Romano to a one-year, $2 million contract during the offseason. Compare that to the $8.5 million the Phillies paid just one year earlier, and you start to understand the value proposition.

The early returns have been nothing short of spectacular:

  • 6 appearances without allowing a single hit
  • 4 saves in 4 opportunities (he’s already halfway to his 2025 full-season total)
  • 7 strikeouts against just 2 walks
  • 0.00 ERA and a minuscule 0.40 WHIP
  • Ranks among the top 10 MLB pitchers in WHIP among those with at least 5.0 innings pitched

For perspective, it took Romano until May 14th to record his fourth save with the Phillies last season. With the Angels, he reached that mark in less than a month.

Romano also joins fellow former Blue Jay Yusei Kikuchi on the Angels roster, giving Los Angeles a pair of ex-Toronto arms contributing in meaningful roles. The reunion has seemingly created a comfortable environment for the veteran reliever.

What This Means for the Angels — and for Fantasy Baseball

The Angels haven’t made the postseason since 2014. That’s a franchise that had Shohei Ohtani AND Mike Trout together for six seasons and still couldn’t break through. But having a lockdown closer changes the math for a rebuilding club.

Romano’s resurgence has caught the attention of fantasy baseball analysts as well. NBC Sports recently highlighted him as a valuable waiver wire pickup for managers chasing saves, and the New York Post listed him alongside Lucas Erceg and Paul Sewald as relievers who can anchor a fantasy bullpen.

For those in deeper leagues, the key question is sustainability. Five innings in mid-April is a small sample, and Romano is pitching for a team unlikely to contend. But closer opportunities should remain steady, and his underlying metrics suggest this isn’t just a fluke.

What to watch for next: Toronto will face Romano, Kikuchi, and the Angels for a three-game series from April 20 to 22 during the Blue Jays’ first west coast swing. It’ll be a homecoming of sorts — the Canadian closer facing his former club with something to prove.

The Bigger Picture: Why Romano’s Story Matters

Jordan Romano’s comeback isn’t just a feel-good baseball story. It’s a case study in how traditional stats can obscure the truth about a player’s ability.

Teams that relied solely on ERA to evaluate Romano saw a washed-up closer. Teams that dug into expected metrics, pitch tracking data, and injury context saw a two-time All-Star who was due for positive regression.

The Angels bet $2 million on the latter interpretation. So far, that bet is looking like one of the smartest moves of the entire offseason.

At 32, Romano still has years of productive baseball ahead of him if his arm stays healthy. A dominant 2026 could set him up for a multi-year deal next winter — turning a bargain-bin signing into a springboard for the next chapter of his career.

The question now is: can Romano sustain this dominance over a full season, or will the regression monsters eventually catch up? If his advanced metrics are any guide, there’s real reason to believe this version of Jordan Romano is closer to the All-Star we remember than the struggling reliever teams gave up on.
