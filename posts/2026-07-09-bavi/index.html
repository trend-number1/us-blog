Typhoon Bavi: The Category 5 Monster Storm Explained

July 9, 2026 18:03 Trend Insights

A wall of wind moving at 180 mph just did something no storm has ever done to the tiny island of Rota. Typhoon Bavi became the first Category 5 system on record to send its eye directly over the island north of Guam.

That single fact tells you why meteorologists across the Pacific are watching this storm so closely right now.

Quick Summary

  • Typhoon Bavi struck Guam and the U.S. Northern Mariana Islands at Category 5 intensity with winds near 180 mph.
  • It was the first Category 5 storm whose eye passed directly over Rota, surpassing Category 4 Gilda from 1967.
  • The system is now tracking toward Taiwan and eastern China, where floods have already killed 39 people.
  • Forecasters say Bavi could be Taiwan’s strongest storm since 2024.

Why Typhoon Bavi Became a Monster

Super Typhoon Bavi (known by the Philippine name Inday) roared into Category 5 strength early on a Saturday, and forecasters warned it could grow even stronger before reaching land.

The National Weather Service Forecast Office in Guam clocked maximum sustained winds climbing to 160 mph, up from a Category 4 ranking just hours earlier. Winds later peaked near 180 mph in the eyewall.

Here is the part most people miss: typhoons and hurricanes are the same phenomenon. Storms in the western Pacific north of the equator are simply called typhoons instead of hurricanes.

Bavi’s minimum central pressure dropped to 945 hPa, and it generated maximum significant wave heights of 15.2 meters (about 50 feet). Those are open-ocean waves as tall as a five-story building.

So how much damage can a storm like this actually cause on land? The islands found out the hard way.

The Direct Hit on Guam and the Marianas

Bavi raked across Rota, about 60 miles northeast of Guam, at Category 5 intensity. The storm struck with peak sustained winds near 180 mph in the eyewall.

This was historic. While Guam and the Northern Marianas have a long history with intense super typhoons, Bavi appeared to be the first Category 5 whose eye passed directly over Rota. Only Category 4 Gilda, back on November 13, 1967, had come close.

The damage was widespread, especially on Rota:

  • A water distribution station near Rota’s airport suffered structural damage.
  • Power poles and lines were downed and bent at the airport itself.
  • A wood structure with a tin roof was reportedly destroyed.

Wind gusts were brutal across the region. Saipan International Airport and the National Weather Service in Guam both recorded gusts of 111 mph, while the island of Tinian saw a gust to 94 mph.

Science and operations officer Brandon Aydlett had urged residents to act early. “Prepare today for this approaching super typhoon,” he told The Guam Daily Post.

He also offered a warning that applies to any storm forecast: “Don’t look at that center forecast track line, because it can and it will shift ever so slightly north or south.” Any of the four main islands, he stressed, could see direct impacts.

That unpredictability is exactly why the next phase of Bavi’s journey matters so much.

Where Typhoon Bavi Is Heading Next

After slamming the Marianas, Bavi continued west and northwest, tracking more than 500 miles southeast of Okinawa. At one recent fix, it sat roughly 876 km (about 544 miles) south of Kadena Air Base.

The super typhoon Bavi track now points toward Taiwan, with the system expected to brush the island before heading into eastern China late this week.

Forecasters expect Bavi to slowly weaken over the coming days, but it will remain a typhoon. A weakening storm is still a dangerous one, particularly for rainfall.

Here is the insight that should stop you scrolling: the biggest threat from many landfalling typhoons is not wind at all. It is water. Taiwan and China are already dealing with flooding that has killed 39 people, and Bavi’s heavy rain threatens to make that far worse.

Taipei Times reporting suggests Bavi could be Taiwan’s strongest storm since 2024. Flights and ferries to remote islands have already been canceled as the storm approaches.

How does a Category 5 typhoon actually stack up against the storms most Americans know? A quick comparison helps.

How Bavi Compares to Familiar Storms

Understanding wind categories makes the danger concrete. The Saffir-Simpson scale used for hurricanes applies the same way to typhoons.

At its peak, Bavi’s winds sat well above the Category 5 threshold. For perspective, sustained winds of 180 mph can strip roofs, flatten wood-frame structures, and knock out power for weeks.

The humanitarian response is already mobilizing. Red Cross volunteers heading to help after Super Typhoon Bavi staged in Hawaii on their way to the affected islands.

What This Means For You

Even if you live thousands of miles from the Pacific, Bavi is a useful case study in storm preparedness.

  1. Never trust a single forecast line. As Aydlett warned, tracks shift. Prepare for a wider cone of impact.
  2. Water often beats wind. Flooding kills more people than wind in many storms. Know your flood risk.
  3. Act early, not late. The window to prepare closes fast once a storm nears land.

One common misconception is that a “weakening” storm is safe. Bavi is forecast to weaken yet still deliver dangerous rain to Taiwan and China. Intensity on paper does not equal the full threat on the ground.

The Bottom Line on Typhoon Bavi

Typhoon Bavi has already earned a place in the record books by driving a Category 5 eye directly over Rota for the first time. Now it turns toward Taiwan and China, where millions brace for what could be the most powerful storm in years.

The storm is a stark reminder that the Pacific typhoon season can produce systems of staggering power, and that preparation saves lives when the forecast line shifts.

If a storm of this magnitude were bearing down on your community with 48 hours of warning, would you know exactly what to do first?
