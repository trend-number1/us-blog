Amanda Anisimova: The Surprising Comeback Rewriting Tennis

July 3, 2026 00:03 Trend Insights

What if the worst 57 minutes of your career became the turning point that made you great? For Amanda Anisimova, that is not a hypothetical — it is the story of the last twelve months.

In 2025, the American lost the Wimbledon final by a stunning 6-0 6-0 scoreline, the first such result in a Wimbledon final in over a century. Six weeks later, she beat the very player who beat her. This is how one of tennis’s most remarkable comebacks actually happened.

Quick Summary:

  • Amanda Anisimova finished 2025 at a career-high No. 4 ranking
  • She reached her first two Grand Slam finals (Wimbledon and US Open) in a single season
  • She won her first two WTA 1000 titles at Doha and Beijing
  • In 2023 she stepped away from tennis at just 21 to protect her mental health
  • Her biggest weapon may no longer be her backhand — it’s her mindset

From Teen Prodigy to a Necessary Reset

Amanda Anisimova was never short on talent. Back in 2019, at just 17 years old, she reached the semifinals of Roland Garros — becoming the first player, man or woman, born in the 2000s to advance to that stage of a major.

The rise looked unstoppable. But the years that followed brought professional and personal setbacks that took a real toll.

In 2023, at only 21, she made the brave decision to step away from the sport entirely. Her reason was refreshingly honest.

“Ultimately, tennis wasn’t something that was bringing me any joy at that point,” she has said. “I had experienced some professional and personal setbacks that contributed to feelings of anxiety and depression, and I knew I needed a reset.”

Many doubted whether the former teen prodigy could ever climb back among the world’s best. As we’ll see next, that skepticism only lit the fire.

The 2025 Breakthrough: A Season for the Ages

Anisimova returned to the WTA Tour at the start of 2025 — and came back with a bang.

Consider what she accomplished in a single year:

  • Won her first two career WTA 1000 titles: Doha (defeating Ostapenko in the final) and Beijing (defeating Noskova in the final)
  • Reached her first two Grand Slam finals: Wimbledon (lost to Iga Swiatek) and the US Open (lost to Aryna Sabalenka)
  • Made her Top 20, Top 10, and Top 5 debuts — all in the same season
  • Finished the year at a career-high ranking of No. 4

For context, here is how her recent seasons stack up:

That leap from a mid-season return to the world’s top five in under a year is the kind of trajectory most players never touch. But the number that defines her isn’t a ranking — it’s a scoreline.

The Wimbledon Final Nobody Forgets

Here is the moment that could have broken her.

At the 2025 Wimbledon final, Iga Swiatek did not allow Anisimova a single game. The match lasted just 57 minutes, ending 6-0 6-0. Her then-coach called it “the worst off day we have ever experienced.”

Frozen with nerves on Centre Court, she suffered a defeat that most athletes would replay for years.

Then came the plot twist. Just six weeks later at the US Open, Anisimova defeated Swiatek and marched back into another Grand Slam final. Against Sabalenka she was far from frozen — she was unlucky not to force a deciding set, ultimately losing 6-4 7-6 (7-4).

That refusal to be defined by one catastrophic day is the heart of her story. And it points to what has truly changed in her game.

What Really Changed: Mindset Over Mechanics

Ask fans about Anisimova and many will mention her backhand — widely regarded as one of the best on the WTA Tour. But that weapon has been part of her arsenal for years.

Former WTA title winner Nicole Pratt believes the real difference is mental.

“I think it’s that mental fortitude and belief system she now has,” Pratt said. “That comes through consistency and winning matches. You just can’t click your fingers and it’s there.”

The key insight: she now wins the close ones. Pratt noted that Anisimova is increasingly grinding through three-set battles — coming through the moments where she is most challenged, then stacking the wins.

Anisimova frames it as growth, not grievance. “One match doesn’t erase all the work, growth and success that came before it,” she has said of the Wimbledon loss. “It may be a bumpy ride at times, but it’s still special, and it’s still a joy ride.”

A common misconception is that elite comebacks are about rediscovering lost talent. Anisimova’s case suggests the opposite — the talent was always there. What she rebuilt was the relationship with the sport itself.

What This Means For You

You don’t need a Grand Slam final to take something from this.

Here is the actionable throughline in Anisimova’s story:

  1. A reset is not a retreat. Stepping away in 2023 preserved a career instead of ending it.
  2. One bad day is data, not destiny. She used the 6-0 6-0 loss to learn how to process nerves.
  3. Consistency builds belief. Winning close matches is what forged the confidence, not the other way around.

As she returns to the All England Club in 2026 — having already won early-round matches and sent what one outlet called a “strong warning” to the field — the 24-year-old carries a different outlook on what success even means.

So here’s the question worth sitting with: When your worst day arrives, will you let it define you — or refine you?

Which of your own setbacks might actually be a reset in disguise? That reframe may be the most valuable serve in the whole match.
