Travel Vaccine Alert: CDC Warns of Polio Risk in 32 Countries Before Spring Break

March 12, 2026 16:32 Trend Insights

The last natural polio infection in the United States occurred in 1979. Most Americans have never thought twice about the disease. But on March 3, the CDC issued a Level 2 travel notice covering 32 countries — and some of the names on that list will surprise you.

Key Takeaways

  • The CDC issued a Level 2 travel notice on March 3 for polio, covering 32 countries
  • Advisory includes European nations like the UK, Spain, Finland, Germany, and Poland
  • Poliovirus is actively circulating in Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Europe
  • Adults who were vaccinated as children may need one lifetime booster shot before traveling
  • A separate measles surge is also fueling health warnings ahead of Spring Break
  • The inactivated polio vaccine is available at health departments and primary care offices

If you have international travel plans this spring, this is not something to scroll past. Here is everything you need to know.

Polio in 2026 — Why a “Defeated” Disease Is Back in the Headlines

Many Americans assume polio was eradicated decades ago. That is a common misconception. While the disease has been eliminated domestically — the last natural US case was in 1979 — poliovirus never disappeared from the world.

The CDC’s March 3 advisory confirms that poliovirus is actively circulating in Africa, the Middle East, and now parts of Europe. The list of 32 countries under the Level 2 notice includes destinations that millions of Americans visit every year: the United Kingdom, Spain, Finland, Germany, and Poland, among others.

A Level 2 notice means the CDC recommends travelers “practice enhanced precautions.” In practical terms, that means making sure your polio vaccination is up to date before you board a plane.

This is not a theoretical risk. When poliovirus circulates in a population, it spreads through contaminated food, contaminated water, and improper handwashing — the kinds of exposure that become more likely when you are navigating unfamiliar environments abroad.

So what does polio actually do, and why should healthy adults care?

What Polio Does to the Body — And Why It Is More Dangerous Than You Think

Polio is not just a childhood disease from old newsreels. It is a virus that attacks the nervous system and can cause outcomes ranging from mild flu-like symptoms to permanent disability, paralysis, or death.

Here is what makes polio particularly insidious:

  • Most infected people show no symptoms at all — but they can still spread the virus to others
  • In severe cases, the virus destroys motor neurons in the spinal cord, leading to irreversible paralysis
  • Even people who recover from acute polio can experience “post-polio” symptoms decades later

Dr. Andrew Jameson of Trinity Health has warned that polio can resurface later in life with post-polio syndrome — a condition that causes new muscle weakness, fatigue, and pain in people who had polio years or even decades earlier.

“People assume that if they survived polio as a child, it is behind them,” Dr. Jameson noted. But post-polio symptoms demonstrate that the virus can leave lasting damage to the nervous system that manifests long after the initial infection.

The One Shot That Could Save Your Trip — And Your Health

Here is the good news: the inactivated polio vaccine (IPV) is safe, widely available, and highly effective. Most Americans received the polio vaccine series as children. But if you are traveling internationally to any of the 32 countries on the CDC advisory, you may need one additional lifetime booster shot.

The CDC’s recommendation is clear: be up to date on your polio vaccine before international travel.

Here is what that looks like in practice:

  • If you completed the childhood vaccine series: You may receive one lifetime booster dose of IPV before traveling to affected areas
  • If you are unsure of your vaccination history: Talk to your doctor — they can check records or recommend a catch-up schedule
  • Where to get it: The inactivated polio vaccine is available at health departments and primary care offices across the country

This is not a complicated or expensive process. A single appointment at your primary care doctor or local health department is all it takes. The real risk is not knowing you need it and traveling unprotected.

But polio is not the only disease travelers should be thinking about right now.

Spring Break Double Threat: Measles Surge Adds Urgency

The CDC’s polio warning comes at a time when measles cases are also surging, adding a second layer of concern for anyone planning Spring Break travel.

Measles, like polio, was once considered a problem of the past in the United States. But declining vaccination rates in some communities have opened the door for outbreaks — both domestically and internationally. Health officials are now issuing parallel warnings about both diseases as millions of Americans prepare to travel in the coming weeks.

The timing is critical:

  • Spring Break peaks in mid-to-late March, with millions traveling internationally
  • Measles is one of the most contagious diseases known — it can linger in the air for up to two hours after an infected person leaves a room
  • Both polio and measles vaccines are routine childhood immunizations, but adults may need boosters or catch-up doses

If you are planning international travel, the smartest move is to schedule a pre-travel health appointment and review all your vaccinations — not just polio.

Beyond Polio and Measles: Other Travel Health Alerts

The CDC’s travel health notices extend beyond polio. Notably, a chikungunya outbreak has been identified in France’s Indian Ocean Department, adding yet another destination-specific risk for travelers heading to French overseas territories.

Chikungunya is a mosquito-borne virus that causes fever and severe joint pain. While rarely fatal, it can cause debilitating symptoms that last for weeks or months. There is no specific treatment, so prevention through mosquito bite avoidance is the primary defense.

This reinforces a broader point: travel health risks are not limited to developing countries. European nations, popular tourist destinations, and well-developed healthcare systems are all seeing the re-emergence of diseases that many assumed were under control.

What This Means for You — A Pre-Travel Checklist

Whether you are heading to London, Berlin, or Cancun this Spring Break, here is what health experts recommend:

  1. Check your vaccination records — Contact your primary care provider and confirm you are up to date on polio, measles, and other routine immunizations
  2. Get a polio booster if needed — Adults may receive one lifetime booster shot of IPV; it is available at health departments and doctor’s offices
  3. Review CDC travel notices — Visit the CDC’s travel health page for destination-specific alerts before booking
  4. Practice food and water safety — Polio spreads through contaminated food and water, so stick to bottled or treated water in affected regions
  5. Wash your hands thoroughly and frequently — Improper handwashing is a key transmission route for poliovirus
  6. Consider travel health insurance — Medical evacuation from overseas can cost tens of thousands of dollars

The cost of prevention is a single doctor’s visit. The cost of ignoring it could be catastrophic.

The Bottom Line: Do Not Let a Preventable Disease Ruin Your Trip

The CDC’s Level 2 polio travel notice for 32 countries is not a reason to cancel your plans. It is a reason to prepare properly. The inactivated polio vaccine is one of the most effective tools in modern medicine, and getting a booster before you travel is fast, simple, and widely accessible.

Polio was never fully eradicated worldwide. Measles is surging. New outbreaks like chikungunya are emerging in unexpected places. The common thread is that vaccines remain the single best defense — and making sure yours are current takes less than an hour.

With Spring Break just days away, have you checked whether your vaccinations are up to date? One appointment now could be the difference between a great trip and a medical emergency abroad.
