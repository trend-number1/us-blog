Quinten Post's $30M Offer Sheet Puts Warriors on the Clock

July 7, 2026 00:03 Trend Insights

A No. 52 draft pick just forced one of the NBA’s smartest front offices into a 36-hour decision worth $30 million.

That is the reality facing the Golden State Warriors this week. Quinten Post, the Dutch stretch big man they plucked late in the 2024 draft, has signed a three-year, $30 million offer sheet with the Memphis Grizzlies. Now the clock is ticking.

Quick Summary

  • Quinten Post signed a 3-year, $30 million offer sheet with the Memphis Grizzlies.
  • As a restricted free agent, the Warriors can match to keep him.
  • Golden State has until 11:59 p.m. ET on Tuesday, July 7 to decide.
  • Reporting suggests a match is unlikely after the team added veteran bigs.

Why Quinten Post’s Offer Sheet Matters

Restricted free agency is one of the NBA’s most fascinating pressure cookers, and the Quinten Post offer sheet is a textbook example of how it works.

Post is a restricted free agent, which means his current team retains the right to match any offer he signs elsewhere. On Monday, ESPN’s Shams Charania reported that Post agreed to a three-year, $30 million contract with the Grizzlies.

Because the deal is an offer sheet rather than a standard signing, it is essentially conditional. If the Warriors match, they keep Post at those exact terms. If they decline, he heads to Memphis.

The mechanics are simple, but the stakes are not. This single decision ripples across Golden State’s cap sheet, rotation, and long-term flexibility.

So how much time does the front office actually have to think it over? Less than you might expect.

The 36-Hour Countdown

Here is where the drama sharpens. According to the reporting, Golden State has until 11:59 p.m. ET on Tuesday, July 7 to match the offer.

That deadline gives the Warriors roughly 36 hours from the moment the offer sheet was first reported by Charania on Monday. They may have had a slightly earlier heads-up, but the window is undeniably narrow.

The way offer sheets work protects a team’s rights while still allowing the player to pursue fair market value elsewhere. In plain terms:

  1. Post signs the offer sheet with the Grizzlies.
  2. The Warriors get a matching window to keep him at those terms.
  3. If they match, Post stays in Golden State on the three-year, $30 million deal.
  4. If they pass, Post becomes a Grizzly.

For a front office that has to weigh dozens of moving pieces, 36 hours is barely enough time to run the numbers. And those numbers may already point toward a difficult goodbye.

What the Warriors Are Weighing

Reporting indicates a match is unlikely — and the reasons are rooted in moves Golden State already made this offseason.

The team has signed veteran bigs Kristaps Porziņģis and Al Horford to new contracts this offseason. With frontcourt minutes and money already committed, absorbing another $30 million over three years for a developing center becomes a harder sell.

That does not make the decision painless. Post was a genuine draft success story, and letting a homegrown contributor walk always stings.

Consider what Golden State would be giving up. Here is a snapshot of the situation:

The Warriors already extended a Qualifying Offer to Post — alongside Taran Armstrong and Nico Mannion — back on June 29, which is what preserved their matching rights in the first place. So this is not a case of overlooking a player. It is a case of a crowded frontcourt colliding with a rising price tag.

Which raises the real question: was Post actually worth matching? The numbers tell an interesting story.

The Numbers Behind the Dutch Big Man

Golden State selected Post with the No. 52 pick in the 2024 NBA Draft, and the Dutch big man quickly carved out a role as an intriguing stretch big in coach Steve Kerr’s system.

Across 109 career games (49 starts) over two seasons, Post posted these averages:

  • 7.8 points per game
  • 3.8 rebounds per game
  • 1.4 assists per game
  • 44.3 percent shooting from the field
  • 36.4 percent from 3-point range
  • 16.9 minutes per game

For a late second-round pick, that is meaningful production. A center who can space the floor at better than 36 percent from deep is exactly the kind of modern big that contending teams covet.

That shooting touch is precisely why the Grizzlies were willing to commit real money. In today’s NBA, floor-spacing bigs are a premium asset, and Memphis clearly sees upside worth $10 million a year.

A common misconception is that offer sheets are a formality once a player signs one. They are not. The original team holds all the leverage during the matching window — the player cannot negotiate elsewhere until the clock runs out. Post’s future is genuinely in Golden State’s hands, not his own, until Tuesday night.

So what should you actually watch for as this unfolds?

What This Means for You

If you follow the NBA — or Golden State specifically — this is a signal about the franchise’s direction.

By prioritizing established veterans like Porziņģis and Horford over a younger, cheaper option, the Warriors appear to be leaning into a win-now window rather than developmental patience. Reading roster moves this way helps you anticipate a team’s next steps.

Here is actionable advice for tracking situations like this:

  • Watch the deadline, not the rumor. Offer sheets are decided at the buzzer; early “unlikely to match” reports can flip.
  • Follow the cap math. New veteran contracts often explain why a team lets a young player walk.
  • Value the skill, not the draft slot. A No. 52 pick shooting 36.4 percent from three is a reminder that late-round gems reshape rosters.

For Memphis fans, this could be the addition of a versatile floor-spacing center. For Warriors fans, it may be the bittersweet loss of a draft-day steal.

The bigger takeaway is that in the modern NBA, restricted free agency is where quiet roster philosophies become public decisions. A single offer sheet can reveal exactly how a front office values youth, shooting, and financial flexibility all at once.

So here is the question worth sitting with: if you ran the Warriors, would you spend $30 million to keep a 36-percent shooter you found at pick No. 52 — or let him go and trust the veterans you just paid? The answer says a lot about how you’d build a contender.
