Todd Blanche’s Bold DOJ Audition: What’s Really Happening

Imagine being told your new job is technically temporary — but the boss calls it an audition. That’s the position Todd Blanche finds himself in right now, and he’s not playing it safe. In just weeks since stepping in as Acting Attorney General, the former personal attorney to President Donald Trump has launched a flurry of headline-grabbing moves at the Department of Justice.

Quick Summary:

  • Todd Blanche became Acting AG after Pam Bondi was ousted
  • Trump told him to treat the role as an audition for the permanent job
  • He’s reclassified medical marijuana, restored firing squads and electrocution as federal execution methods, and indicted the Southern Poverty Law Center
  • Critics say he’s politicizing the DOJ; supporters call it overdue accountability

Whether you cheer or recoil, one thing is clear: the Justice Department is moving fast, and the next few months will define the post-Bondi era.

Who Is Todd Blanche, and Why Now?

Blanche was named Acting Attorney General earlier this month after President Trump fired Pam Bondi. According to reporting, Trump grew frustrated with what he viewed as a lack of progress under Bondi on prosecuting his political adversaries.

Blanche, who previously served as Trump’s personal defense attorney, told the president he wanted the job permanently. Trump’s response, as reported by Fox News, was direct: consider this an audition.

That framing matters. It explains the speed and visibility of Blanche’s early actions — every press conference, indictment, and policy reversal is also a job interview.

At his first news conference, Blanche praised Bondi as “a great patriot” and said the two remain close allies. But his style is already different. Rather than letting aides manage reporters, Blanche took more than a dozen questions himself, projecting what observers described as a more combative, hands-on presence.

So what has he actually done? The list is longer than you’d expect for someone barely settled into the job.

The Big Moves: Marijuana, Executions, and the SPLC

Three actions in particular have dominated headlines, and each signals a distinct priority.

1. Reclassifying Medical Marijuana

On April 23, Blanche signed an order reclassifying state-licensed medical marijuana as a less dangerous drug. The move, reported by ABC News, CNN, and NBC News, eases longstanding federal restrictions and aligns with a years-long push to update the Controlled Substances Act framework.

For patients, dispensaries, and state regulators, this is potentially seismic. It doesn’t fully legalize cannabis federally, but it lowers the legal stakes for state-licensed medical use.

2. Bringing Back Firing Squads and Electrocution

In a move that drew sharp criticism, the DOJ announced it would once again use firing squads and electrocution for federal executions, framing it as a way to “strengthen the death penalty.” Coverage from CNBC and The Independent emphasized the historical weight of the decision — methods rarely used at the federal level for decades are returning to the table.

3. Indicting the Southern Poverty Law Center

The DOJ indicted the Southern Poverty Law Center (SPLC) on fraud charges, alleging the group paid roughly $3 million between 2014 and 2023 to people associated with extremist organizations including the United Klans of America. Charges include wire fraud, false statements to a bank, and conspiracy to commit money laundering.

Blanche, speaking on Fox News’ “The Ingraham Angle,” called the alleged conduct “extraordinarily egregious” and rejected the idea that the indictment was politically motivated. Critics, including Mother Jones, see it as a sign the DOJ is now actively targeting groups associated with the political left.

These three actions, taken together, paint a picture of a DOJ moving in multiple ideologically charged directions at once — and that’s before we get to personnel.

Personnel and Priorities: Reading Between the Lines

Less than two weeks into his tenure, Blanche oversaw the firing of four career prosecutors following a report that accused them of unfairly punishing anti-abortion protesters. That report was widely criticized, but the firings stuck.

He also brought in Joe diGenova, an 81-year-old former U.S. attorney and longtime Trump ally, to oversee an investigation into former CIA director John Brennan and others tied to the 2016 Russia interference assessment. DiGenova previously represented Trump’s 2020 campaign in efforts to challenge the election results.

At his first press conference, Blanche said the DOJ is currently handling more than 8,000 fraud matters nationwide, highlighting recent guilty pleas in healthcare and COVID-19 fraud cases totaling more than half a billion dollars. The fraud focus is real — but it’s competing with everything else for the spotlight.

That collision between traditional law enforcement and politically charged actions is exactly what makes this moment so unusual.

The Big Debate: Independence vs. Direction

Here’s where it gets contentious. When asked whether he’d use the DOJ to more aggressively target Trump’s perceived enemies, Blanche pushed back at his press conference.

He acknowledged that “some” ongoing investigations involve people the president has had public disputes with, but framed it as the president’s “right” and “duty” to want them investigated. He explicitly said he does not view Trump’s public statements as “pressure” on him.

Critics aren’t convinced. The Guardian wrote that Blanche has “left little doubt” about how he’d further politicize the department if confirmed. Common concerns include:

  • The pace and visibility of politically sensitive actions
  • The hiring of explicitly partisan figures for sensitive probes
  • The firing of career prosecutors after a contested internal report
  • The reopening of investigations into former officials like Brennan

Supporters counter that prior administrations also pursued politically sensitive cases, and that the SPLC indictment, fraud crackdown, and marijuana reclassification show genuine breadth — not just retribution.

The truth, as usual, is messier than either narrative. And it’s about to matter to a lot more people than political junkies.

What This Means For You

Why should the average reader care? Three reasons:

  1. Marijuana reclassification could affect millions of patients, dispensary owners, and investors in legal cannabis markets. State-licensed medical use just became less federally fraught.
  2. Federal execution policy shifts how the United States carries out its most serious punishment — a significant moral and legal change.
  3. DOJ priorities ripple outward into white-collar enforcement, civil rights cases, and how aggressively federal prosecutors pursue fraud (currently 8,000+ active matters).

Common misconception: that an “acting” official is a placeholder. Blanche’s tenure proves the opposite. Acting officials can — and clearly do — make decisions with real, lasting impact.

If you’re a small business owner, watch the fraud enforcement push. If you work in healthcare, the half-billion-dollar pleas this month signal heightened scrutiny. If you follow civil liberties, the SPLC case and execution-method changes are worth tracking closely.

The Bottom Line

Todd Blanche took the most powerful law enforcement job in America with explicit instructions to audition for it. So far, he’s auditioned loudly — through high-profile indictments, contested personnel moves, dramatic policy reversals, and an unusually public communication style.

Whether you see this as overdue reform or worrying politicization likely depends on where you started. But the velocity is undeniable.

The real question: When the audition ends, what kind of Justice Department will be left behind — and how much of it can be undone?

Keep an eye on the next few weeks. In a town where most cabinet transitions move slowly, this one is sprinting.
