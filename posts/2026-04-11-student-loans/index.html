Student Loans 2026: Essential Changes Every Borrower Must Know Before July

April 11, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

The federal student loan landscape is undergoing its most dramatic transformation in decades — and 7.5 million borrowers just received guidance that their repayment plan no longer exists. If you have student loans, the next three months could reshape your financial future.

Key Takeaways

  • The SAVE Plan has been officially eliminated, affecting 7.5 million borrowers
  • The Treasury Department is taking over the $1.7 trillion student loan portfolio
  • Major borrowing and repayment changes take effect July 1, 2026
  • Parent PLUS loan holders had an effective deadline of April 1 to consolidate for IDR eligibility
  • PSLF borrowers face higher “buy-back” payments under new calculation formulas

Student Loans Enter a New Era: The SAVE Plan Is Dead

The U.S. Department of Education has begun issuing guidance to all borrowers enrolled in the now-defunct Saving on a Valuable Education (SAVE) Plan, directing them to exit and enter a legal federal student loan repayment plan.

The SAVE Plan was the Biden Administration’s third and final attempt at mass student loan forgiveness. Federal, district, and appellate courts blocked it repeatedly. According to the Department of Education, the plan would have cost taxpayers more than $342 billion over 10 years.

A court-approved settlement between the Department and the State of Missouri formally ended the program. Under the terms:

  • No new borrowers will be enrolled in the SAVE Plan
  • All pending applications will be denied
  • All current SAVE Plan borrowers will be moved into legal repayment plans

“Today’s guidance puts the Biden Administration’s illegal student loan bailout agenda to rest once and for all,” said Under Secretary of Education Nicholas Kent. The administration’s position is straightforward: if you take out a loan, you must pay it back.

For millions of borrowers who enrolled expecting forgiveness and artificially low monthly payments, the adjustment will be significant. But the changes don’t stop there.

Treasury Takes the Wheel: A $1.7 Trillion Transfer

In what the Department of Education called a “historic step,” the Trump administration is transferring the nearly $1.7 trillion student loan portfolio from the Department of Education to the Treasury Department.

This move is part of a broader effort to wind down the Department of Education, fulfilling a campaign pledge by President Trump. Secretary of Education Linda McMahon framed the decision as a correction to decades of mismanagement.

“With nearly a quarter of student loan borrowers in default, Americans know that the Department of Education has failed to effectively manage and deliver these critical programs,” McMahon stated.

The transfer will first impact roughly 10 million borrowers who have either defaulted on their loans or are in late-stage delinquency. The transition follows a multi-phase process under a new interagency agreement between Education and Treasury.

Critics have raised concerns. Roxanne Garza, director of higher education policy at a consumer advocacy group, has questioned whether Treasury has the infrastructure and borrower-facing systems to manage millions of individual loan accounts. The full impact remains to be seen as the transfer unfolds.

What’s clear is that borrowers can no longer count on the status quo — and the biggest changes arrive this summer.

July 2026: The Rules Rewrite Borrowers Can’t Ignore

President Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the most expansive changes to federal student loan borrowing and repayment in decades. These changes take effect July 1, 2026, and they touch nearly every aspect of the system.

Here’s what’s changing:

  • Fewer repayment options — The current menu of about a half-dozen plans will be significantly narrowed
  • Different loan terms for many borrowers
  • Restructured eligibility for post-graduate financing
  • New rules for consolidation that affect legacy plan access

Who’s grandfathered in? Borrowers who have already taken out loans and do not plan to consolidate them will retain access to some legacy repayment plans. However, new borrowers — and existing borrowers who consolidate or take out additional loans — will be subject to the new rules entirely.

“If you don’t understand it, that’s not your fault. It’s just phenomenally complicated,” said Winston Berkman-Breen, legal director at Protect Borrowers.

The Parent PLUS Deadline That Already Passed

One critical deadline has already come and gone. Parents with PLUS loans were inside a critical processing window leading up to July 1. The recommended three-month consolidation processing buffer meant the effective safe deadline was April 1, 2026.

Applications submitted after that date risk missing the processing window and losing access to income-driven repayment options entirely. If you’re a Parent PLUS borrower who hasn’t acted yet, contact your loan servicer immediately to explore remaining options.

The RISE Initiative: Still in Play

The Department of Education is actively reviewing over 81,838 public comments submitted for the RISE initiative. A Final Rule is expected ahead of the July 1 effective date. Key unresolved issues include the potential reclassification of professional degrees — nursing, social work, physical therapy, and MBA programs are currently excluded from the higher $50,000 annual borrowing cap.

Advocacy groups are pressing for inclusion before the Final Rule is published. Borrowers in these fields should monitor Department of Education press releases closely.

Public Service Loan Forgiveness Just Got Harder

For borrowers pursuing Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF) — the program that forgives student debt for government and nonprofit workers after 10 years of qualifying payments — the elimination of the SAVE Plan has created a painful ripple effect.

PSLF borrowers have the option to “buy back” months spent in deferment or forbearance if those months would push them to the forgiveness threshold. For example, a borrower with nine years of qualifying payments and one year in forbearance could pay off that year’s amount to immediately qualify.

Previously, those buy-back amounts were calculated using the SAVE formula, which allowed for lower monthly payments. Under new guidance, borrowers seeking to buy back months spent in deferment or forbearance on or after July 1, 2024 — when litigation blocked the SAVE Plan — will have their payments calculated using a different, likely higher formula.

This change could increase payments substantially for thousands of borrowers who were counting on SAVE-based calculations to make buy-back affordable.

One Bright Spot: The Forgiveness Tracker Returns

In a piece of positive news, the Department of Education announced that the Student Loan Forgiveness Tracker will be restored. This tool helps borrowers monitor their progress toward forgiveness milestones and had been previously removed, leaving many in the dark about where they stood.

What This Means for You: Action Steps Right Now

The student loan system is being restructured from the ground up. Whether you’re a current borrower, a parent helping with college costs, or a prospective student, here’s what you should do:

  1. Check your repayment plan status — If you were on SAVE, expect guidance from the Department of Education directing you to a new plan
  2. Don’t consolidate without research — Consolidating existing loans will subject you to the new July 2026 rules and could cost you legacy plan access
  3. PSLF borrowers: calculate buy-back costs now — The formula change means waiting could cost you more
  4. Monitor the RISE Final Rule — If you’re in nursing, social work, PT, or MBA programs, the outcome directly affects your borrowing cap
  5. Contact your loan servicer — With the Treasury transition coming, establish communication now while systems are still familiar

The coming months represent a narrow window where informed action can save borrowers thousands of dollars. The rules are changing whether borrowers are ready or not.

With $1.7 trillion in student debt and millions of borrowers facing new repayment realities, one question looms large: will these sweeping changes finally fix a broken system, or will they leave the most vulnerable borrowers further behind?
