Sirius XM Stock Surges 49% as Merger Talks Collapse

July 9, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

Warren Buffett rarely bets big on a struggling company. Yet his Berkshire Hathaway now owns roughly 33.2% of Sirius XM Holdings — a stake it kept building even as the stock cratered 58% in 2024.

That contrarian call is starting to look smart. Sirius XM shares have rallied 49% in recent months, and the company just walked away from a blockbuster merger that could have reshaped American radio.

So what changed? And should the average listener or investor care? Let’s break it down.

Key Takeaways

  • Sirius XM (SIRI) stock has climbed 49% and still looks like a bargain to some analysts.
  • Merger talks with iHeartMedia stalled and fizzled out in June 2026.
  • Berkshire Hathaway holds about 33.2% of the company.
  • Management is refocusing on its core automotive customer with $200M in cost cuts.
  • The company still counts 30 million+ satellite and streaming subscribers.

Why the Sirius XM and iHeartMedia Merger Collapsed

For a few months in 2026, the audio industry braced for a giant. Sirius XM, the only satellite radio broadcaster in the U.S., was in talks to combine parts of its business with iHeartMedia, the largest owner of AM and FM stations in the country.

The discussions were first reported back in April 2026 by Bloomberg and The Wrap. By June, The New York Times reported the talks had cooled and ultimately fizzled out.

The negotiations centered mostly on the two companies’ digital media and connected advertising businesses — not a full corporate merger. iHeartMedia operates more than 860 local AM and FM radio stations nationwide.

One sticking point proved decisive. iHeartMedia and regulators could not agree on how many stations the broadcaster would divest to win antitrust approval, and which ones would go.

Neither company ever officially confirmed the talks. As one report noted, since the discussions were never acknowledged, there was nothing to formally deny when they collapsed.

Why does this matter? A combined entity might have controlled a massive share of the U.S. audio market — streamlining content, ad sales, and audience data. Instead, both firms now face the streaming era alone. And that fight is only getting harder, as we’ll see next.

The Streaming Squeeze: Sirius XM vs. the Competition

Traditional radio has been bleeding for two decades. Advertising dollars keep migrating toward streaming music and podcasts, and consumers increasingly reach for apps instead of dials.

Sirius XM’s biggest threats today aren’t other broadcasters — they’re Spotify and Apple Music. Both offer cheaper, phone-first listening that a price-sensitive consumer already has installed.

That’s the core problem management has admitted. Trying to sell a relatively expensive satellite subscription to smartphone-only users hasn’t gone as planned.

Here’s how the major players stack up in the modern audio landscape:

Both Sirius XM and iHeartMedia have poured money into podcasts to evolve beyond their traditional formats. But podcasting is a fiercely contested space where scale and exclusive shows decide the winners.

This is where Sirius XM’s unique assets come into play — and why some investors still see value.

What’s Powering the Turnaround

Despite the pressure, Sirius XM has one thing streamers struggle to replicate: exclusive, can’t-get-it-elsewhere content.

The company remains the home of radio personality Howard Stern, who joined in 2006 and helped build a base of more than 30 million satellite and streaming subscribers.

It keeps signing marquee names and events. Recent examples include a dedicated Morgan Wallen Radio channel and presale access to the Eagles’ December 2026 shows at Sphere in Las Vegas.

On the financial side, management laid out a disciplined plan. In a December 2024 outlook, it guided for lower revenue and adjusted EBITDA — but higher free cash flow.

The strategy boils down to three moves:

  1. Refocus marketing on the core automotive customer, Sirius XM’s traditional stronghold.
  2. Cut costs by an additional $200 million to protect margins.
  3. Prioritize cash flow over chasing unprofitable smartphone-only growth.

Here’s the insight many headlines miss: a stock that fell 58% in 2024 and then rallied 49% isn’t necessarily “fixed.” It’s being repriced as investors weigh whether the cash-flow story is durable.

That distinction matters enormously for anyone thinking about the stock — which brings us to the practical takeaway.

What This Means For You

If you’re a listener, the collapsed merger is arguably good news for choice. Two independent audio giants competing separately tend to invest more aggressively in shows, channels, and podcasts to keep you subscribed.

If you’re an investor, temper the excitement of that 49% run. Sirius XM shed subscribers, revenue, and profit through 2024 — the turnaround thesis rests on free cash flow and cost discipline, not renewed growth.

A few practical points to keep in mind:

  • Watch free cash flow, not just the headline stock move — that’s the metric management is steering toward.
  • Note the Berkshire stake. A 33.2% position from Buffett’s firm signals conviction, but even Berkshire has been wrong before.
  • Track subscriber trends. If the automotive-first refocus stabilizes the subscriber base, the story strengthens.

One common misconception worth clearing up: Sirius XM’s rally does not mean the streaming threat is gone. Spotify and Apple Music aren’t going anywhere, and podcasting competition is intensifying.

The company is separately dealing with a lawsuit alleging its logo refresh copied a movie news site’s branding — a reminder that even brand decisions carry legal risk in a crowded market.

So here’s the real question: In a world where music lives on your phone for free, can exclusive talent and in-car loyalty keep 30 million people paying? Berkshire is betting yes. What’s your call?
