Alaska Air Near-Collision at Newark: Essential Facts About the Alarming Close Call

March 23, 2026 18:03 Trend Insights

Have you ever wondered just how close two massive jets can get before disaster strikes? On March 18, 2026, the answer was a terrifying 300 feet — roughly the length of a football field — when an Alaska Airlines Boeing 737 and a FedEx Boeing 777 cargo plane nearly collided at Newark Liberty International Airport.

Key Takeaways

  • An Alaska Airlines 737 and FedEx 777 came within 300–325 feet of each other on intersecting runways at Newark Airport on March 18, 2026.
  • Air traffic control instructed the Alaska flight to perform a “go-around” when the plane was just 150 feet off the ground.
  • The NTSB and FAA are both investigating the incident.
  • Newark’s intersecting runway design has been flagged as a recurring safety challenge.
  • Separately, Alaska Airlines is scaling up for its busiest spring break operation ever.

Alaska Air and FedEx: What Happened at Newark

The incident occurred around 8:17 p.m. on Tuesday, March 18, as Alaska Airlines Flight 294 arrived from Portland, Oregon, and FedEx Flight 721 arrived from Memphis, Tennessee. Both planes were attempting to land on intersecting runways at one of the busiest airports in the New York City area.

According to radar data from FlightRadar24, air traffic control told the Alaska Airlines pilots to perform a go-around — discontinuing their landing approach and circling for a new attempt — when the 737 was just 150 feet above the ground, seconds before touchdown.

Meanwhile, the FedEx cargo plane continued its landing on the intersecting runway. Preliminary FlightRadar24 data shows the Alaska plane cleared the FedEx jet by just 300 to 325 feet.

The Federal Aviation Administration confirmed the sequence of events: “An air traffic controller instructed Alaska Airlines Flight 294 to perform a go-around at Newark International Airport because FedEx Flight 721 was cleared for the final approach to an intersecting runway.”

So what does this mean for the millions of passengers who fly through Newark every year?

NTSB Investigation and Safety Concerns

Both the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) and the FAA are now investigating the close call. This is standard procedure for runway incursion events, but the razor-thin margins involved have raised serious questions about Newark’s operational safety.

Former FAA official Michael McCormick pointed directly to the airport’s intersecting runway design as the root cause. He explained that timing landings on crossing runways is an inherently difficult task for tower controllers.

“It is a challenge for a tower controller to try to get that timing perfect. It doesn’t always work, and that’s what happened in this case,” McCormick said. In his assessment, the controller waited too long before issuing the go-around instruction, forcing the Alaska Airlines crew into a last-second abort.

How Close Calls Are Classified

Not all near-misses are equal. Aviation authorities categorize runway incidents by severity:

CategoryDescriptionThis Incident
Category AExtreme risk of collisionUnder investigation
Category BSignificant potential for collisionPossible classification
Category CSufficient time/distance to avoidLess likely
Category DLittle or no riskNot applicable

The official classification will come from the NTSB investigation, but the 300-foot separation and the 150-foot altitude at the time of the go-around suggest this was a serious event.

Both airlines emphasized that their crews followed proper protocol. Alaska Airlines stated that “air traffic control issued a go around to our aircraft, which our pilots are highly trained for.” FedEx confirmed that its flight crew “followed instructions from air traffic control and landed safely without incident.”

The professional response from both cockpits likely prevented what could have been a catastrophic outcome. But should passengers be worried about Newark?

Newark Airport’s Intersecting Runway Problem

Newark Liberty International Airport has long been one of the most operationally challenging airports in the United States. Its runway layout — with intersecting paths that require precise sequencing — demands flawless coordination from air traffic controllers.

Here’s what makes Newark uniquely difficult:

  • Two intersecting runways require controllers to time arrivals and departures with split-second precision
  • High traffic volume — Newark handles hundreds of flights daily as a major hub
  • Proximity to other airports — JFK and LaGuardia create one of the most congested airspace corridors in the world
  • Weather variability — wind shifts can force last-minute runway changes

This isn’t the first time Newark has seen close calls. The airport’s design has been a known concern for aviation safety experts for years. The question now is whether the NTSB investigation will lead to operational changes or new safety protocols for intersecting runway operations.

For travelers, the important context is that go-arounds are a standard and safe procedure. Pilots train extensively for exactly this scenario, and the system worked as designed — even if the margins were uncomfortably thin.

Speaking of Alaska Airlines operations, the carrier has much bigger plans on the horizon.

Alaska Airlines’ Record Spring Break Expansion

While the Newark incident made headlines, Alaska Airlines is simultaneously ramping up for what it calls its busiest spring break operation ever. Together with Hawaiian Airlines — now part of the Alaska Air Group — the combined carrier is expanding both its domestic and global network.

According to Alaska Airlines’ own announcement from March 17, 2026, the airline is adding more seats and routes across its system to meet surging spring travel demand.

Recent Route Additions

Alaska Airlines also announced a new route between Santa Rosa, California (in Sonoma County), and Ontario in Southern California, further expanding its West Coast connectivity.

This expansion comes as the airline integrates Hawaiian Airlines into its operations, creating a larger combined network that spans the continental U.S., Hawaii, and international destinations.

But it hasn’t all been smooth flying for the airline this week.

Brenda Song’s First-Class Seat Controversy

Adding to Alaska Airlines’ turbulent week, actress Brenda Song publicly called out the airline on March 22, 2026, for giving up her family’s first-class seats with “no warning.”

The incident, reported by People.com, highlights ongoing concerns about airline overbooking practices and how carriers handle seat reassignments — particularly for families traveling with children. While the full details of Song’s experience are still emerging, it has drawn significant attention to Alaska Airlines’ customer service policies.

What This Means for You

If you’re an Alaska Airlines passenger or planning to fly through Newark, here’s what you should know:

  1. Go-arounds are safe — They are a trained, practiced procedure designed to prevent exactly the kind of disaster that almost happened
  2. The investigation is ongoing — The NTSB’s findings could lead to new safety protocols at Newark
  3. Alaska Airlines is growing — More routes and capacity mean more options for travelers this spring
  4. Know your rights — If you’re involuntarily bumped or your seat is reassigned, airlines are required to follow DOT compensation rules

The Newark near-collision is a stark reminder that aviation safety depends on multiple layers of protection working together — from runway design to air traffic control to pilot training. When one layer falters, the others must catch it.

As the NTSB investigation unfolds, one question lingers: Is Newark’s intersecting runway layout still viable for today’s traffic volumes, or is it time for a fundamental redesign? The answer could shape the future of one of America’s busiest airports.
