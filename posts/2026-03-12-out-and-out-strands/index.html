Today’s puzzle is a vocabulary test disguised as a word search. Here is everything you need to know to solve it — or to understand why you got stuck.

What “Out-and-Out” Actually Means — And Why It Matters

The phrase “out-and-out” means something done completely, without reservation or ambiguity. An out-and-out lie is not a half-truth. An out-and-out victory is not a narrow win. The phrase carries a sense of absolute certainty — and that is exactly the thread connecting every hidden word in today’s grid.

Each of the five theme words is a synonym for “absolute” or “complete”:

  • TOTAL — as in “a total disaster”
  • UTTER — as in “utter nonsense”
  • THOROUGH — as in “a thorough investigation”
  • COMPLETE — as in “a complete transformation”
  • VERITABLE — as in “a veritable goldmine”

These are all words you would use to emphasize that something is unquestionably, undeniably the thing you are describing. Once you see the pattern, the puzzle transforms from cryptic to obvious.

But the real challenge today is the spangram. Let’s break that down.

The Spangram: DYEDINTHEWOOL — A Hidden Idiom Spanning the Grid

Today’s spangram is DYEDINTHEWOOL, a 13-letter phrase made up of four words: “dyed in the wool.” This idiom means someone whose beliefs, habits, or characteristics are deeply ingrained and unchanging — an uncompromising, absolute version of something.

The phrase originates from the textile industry. When yarn is dyed before being woven into fabric, the color is more permanent and resistant to fading than fabric dyed after weaving. A “dyed-in-the-wool” quality is one that goes all the way through — it is not superficial, not temporary, and not negotiable.

In the context of this puzzle, “dyed in the wool” fits perfectly alongside TOTAL, UTTER, THOROUGH, COMPLETE, and VERITABLE. All of these words describe something that is unqualified and absolute. A dyed-in-the-wool optimist is not sometimes optimistic — they are optimistic through and through.

The spangram stretches diagonally across the 6x8 grid, touching both sides. This diagonal path is what makes it particularly tricky to spot. Most solvers instinctively scan horizontally and vertically first, which means the spangram can hide in plain sight for several frustrating minutes.

This constraint is actually your most powerful solving tool. More on that below.

Strategy Guide: How to Solve Tricky Strands Puzzles Like #739

Today’s puzzle is a good example of a grid where strategy matters more than vocabulary. Here are the techniques that experienced Strands solvers use.

Start With the Theme, Not the Grid

Many solvers make the mistake of scanning the grid for any recognizable word. This approach wastes time because you will find plenty of words that are not answers. Instead, decode the theme first.

“Out-and-Out” should make you think of synonyms for “absolute” or “complete.” Once you have that mental list — total, utter, complete, thorough, sheer, veritable, downright — you can scan the grid with purpose instead of wandering.

Use the Every-Letter Rule as a Compass

Since every letter must be used, count the letters. A 6x8 grid has 48 letters total. Today’s answers account for all 48:

  • TOTAL (5) + UTTER (5) + THOROUGH (8) + COMPLETE (8) + VERITABLE (9) + DYEDINTHEWOOL (13) = 48 letters

If you have found four words and the remaining letters do not form coherent answers, one of your found words is wrong. The letter count never lies.

Hunt the Spangram Early

The spangram must touch two opposite edges of the grid. Today’s spangram runs diagonally, which is the hardest type to spot. If you cannot find it right away, look for unusual letter combinations near the grid edges — the D at one corner and the L at the opposite corner are breadcrumbs leading to DYEDINTHEWOOL.

Watch for Direction Changes

Unlike a traditional word search where words travel in straight lines, Strands letters can snake in any direction, changing course at every step. THOROUGH, for example, might start going right, then turn downward, then angle diagonally. Do not limit yourself to linear scans.

Why Strands #739 Is Harder Than It Looks

This puzzle has a deceptive difficulty curve. The individual theme words — TOTAL, UTTER, COMPLETE — are common English words. You would think they would jump off the grid. But three factors conspire to make this puzzle genuinely challenging:

  1. The spangram is an idiom, not a single word. DYEDINTHEWOOL reads as a wall of letters without spaces. Your brain does not naturally parse “DYEDINTHEWOOL” as four separate words, so it looks like gibberish during a grid scan.

  2. The diagonal spangram disrupts spatial intuition. A horizontal or vertical spangram divides the grid into predictable zones. A diagonal spangram creates irregular triangular regions where the remaining words hide, making systematic scanning harder.

  3. Synonym overlap creates false confidence. Words like SHEER, PURE, STARK, and OUTRIGHT also mean “out-and-out” — but they are not in today’s grid. Solvers who fixate on a wrong synonym can waste significant time hunting for letters that do not connect.

The combination of a multi-word diagonal spangram and a theme built on common synonyms makes #739 a solidly mid-to-hard puzzle in the Strands difficulty spectrum.

What Makes a Great Strands Theme

Looking at today’s puzzle alongside recent grids, a pattern emerges in what makes Strands themes satisfying versus frustrating. The best themes share two qualities: the title is a genuine clue (not just a vague gesture), and the connection between words is tight enough to narrow the field once you understand it.

“Out-and-Out” succeeds on both counts. The title is itself a synonym for the theme words, and the category — words meaning “absolute” or “total” — is specific enough that you can generate candidates before even looking at the grid. Compare that to themes where the title is a pun or a misdirection, and today’s puzzle feels fair but challenging — the sweet spot for a daily word game.

The addition of DYEDINTHEWOOL as the spangram elevates the puzzle from a simple synonym exercise to something more layered. It introduces an idiom that most people know but few would think of unprompted, and it forces solvers to recognize a phrase without spaces or capitalization. That is clever puzzle design.

If #739 gave you trouble, tomorrow’s theme might be more intuitive — or it might be even trickier. The best preparation is simply playing consistently and building your mental library of theme patterns and spangram structures.

Did you solve today’s Strands without hints, or did “Out-and-Out” have you reaching for the hint button? The diagonal spangram alone makes this one of the more memorable recent puzzles — and a good reminder that sometimes the answer is hiding right across the middle of the grid, just not in the direction you expected.
