TSA Agents Government Shutdown: The Airport Crisis Explained

March 27, 2026 18:03 Trend Insights

What happens when 480 airport security officers quit and thousands more stop showing up to work — all while millions of Americans are trying to fly? That’s not a hypothetical. It’s happening right now across U.S. airports as the Department of Homeland Security shutdown stretches past 40 days.

Key Takeaways

  • More than 480 TSA officers have quit since the DHS shutdown began on Feb. 14
  • TSA callout rates have more than doubled, from a normal 2% to over 6.1% nationwide
  • Roughly 60,000 TSA employees have gone nearly a month with reduced pay and received $0 paychecks
  • Wait times at some airports have exceeded three hours
  • President Trump ordered DHS to “immediately pay” TSA agents on March 26
  • TSA leadership warns of “long-standing” negative impacts even after funding is restored

TSA Agents Hit a “Breaking Point” as Paychecks Dry Up

The partial government shutdown, triggered when DHS funding lapsed on Feb. 13, has left roughly 60,000 TSA employees working without pay. As of this week, TSA staff prepared to miss a third consecutive paycheck, receiving $0 pay stubs — a gut punch for workers already under intense stress.

“TSA employees say they are at a ‘breaking point,’” the American Federation of Government Employees reported, with union representatives pushing for legislation to ensure federal workers get paid during shutdowns.

The human toll is staggering. USA Today reported that unpaid TSA workers face “exhausting anxiety” as they struggle to cover rent, groceries, and bills. Many have started picking up second jobs or relying on food banks to get by — all while continuing to perform one of the nation’s most critical security functions.

So how did we get here, and what does it mean for your next flight?

Mass Resignations and Record Callouts Are Crippling Airports

The numbers paint a dire picture of an agency in freefall:

  • 480+ TSA officers have outright quit since the shutdown began, according to DHS officials
  • Callout rates surged from a typical 2% to 6.1% nationwide — more than tripling at some hubs
  • More than a third of staff at major airports in Atlanta, Houston, and New York City were absent or called in sick during peak periods
  • Security wait times have ranged from 30 minutes to over three hours at airports including JFK, Hartsfield-Jackson, and Houston’s Hobby Airport

Multiple airports advised travelers to arrive up to three hours early just to clear security. The TSA’s own social media account posted bluntly: “Enough is enough,” citing the crushing wait times.

But the exodus of trained officers could have consequences that outlast the shutdown itself. Jake Rosenfeld, a professor of sociology at Washington University in St. Louis, warned that if TSA agents continue quitting, travel disruptions could persist long after funding is restored.

ICE Deployment: A “Teaspoon of Cough Syrup”

In an effort to shore up airport security, the Trump administration deployed Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers to more than a dozen U.S. airports starting March 23. But there’s a key distinction that has fueled frustration: ICE agents are being paid during the shutdown, thanks to funding secured through last year’s tax and reconciliation bill.

TSA union representatives didn’t hold back in their criticism.

“That’s like giving a person dying of pneumonia a teaspoon of cough syrup,” said Everett Kelley, national president of the American Federation of Government Employees. He argued the ICE deployment does nothing to address the root problem — unpaid TSA officers abandoning their posts.

The pay disparity has drawn sharp scrutiny. Fortune reported that ICE agents can make twice the salary of TSA employees, and economists warned that ICE pay is more “shutdown proof” than other government jobs. PBS highlighted the question many are asking: Why do ICE agents get paid during a shutdown, but not the people screening passengers at airports?

Meanwhile, the White House turned down an offer from Elon Musk to directly pay TSA workers during the shutdown, according to CBS News — a decision that raised even more eyebrows given the desperation on the ground.

Trump Orders TSA Pay — But Recovery Won’t Be Instant

On March 26, President Trump directed DHS to “immediately pay” TSA agents impacted by the government shutdown, according to Scripps News. The BBC reported the order came as travelers continued to face hours-long queues at airports nationwide.

But TSA leadership is sounding alarms that the damage is already done. Deputy Administrator Adam Stahl told Fox News Digital that airports and the agency will suffer “long-standing” negative impacts, even after a spending bill passes.

“The reverberations that will be felt from this will be long-standing,” Stahl said. “They will continue for days after we get a re-appropriation and funding.”

Here’s why the recovery will be slow:

  1. Payroll processing takes time — even with an order to pay, checks don’t arrive overnight
  2. Officers who quit aren’t coming back easily — replacing 480+ trained screeners requires hiring and months of training
  3. Morale is shattered — workers who stayed through weeks of $0 paychecks may still leave once they’re finally paid
  4. Callout patterns have momentum — even after pay resumes, staffing levels won’t normalize immediately

What This Means for You as a Traveler

If you’re flying in the coming days or weeks, here’s what you should know:

  • Arrive early — at least 2–3 hours before domestic flights, 3+ hours for international
  • Check your airport’s wait times before heading out (TSA’s app and airport websites provide real-time updates)
  • Be patient with TSA officers — the workers who are showing up are doing so without pay
  • Consider TSA PreCheck or CLEAR if you don’t already have it — expedited lanes have generally been less impacted
  • Monitor the shutdown status — even after a deal is reached, expect a transition period before normal operations resume

A Common Misconception

Many travelers assume the government shutdown means airports are operating with skeleton crews by choice. The reality is that TSA officers are required to work without pay during shutdowns because they’re deemed “essential.” They’re not choosing to work for free — they legally must, or face termination. The fact that hundreds are quitting anyway underscores just how severe the financial pressure has become.

The Bigger Picture: A System Under Strain

The current DHS shutdown exposes a deeper vulnerability in how the U.S. funds its airport security infrastructure. Democrats and some Republicans have refused to pass the annual DHS budget over concerns about the Trump administration’s immigration enforcement approach — and it’s TSA workers and air travelers who are paying the price.

The partial government shutdown has now become one of the longest in recent memory affecting DHS, and TSA officials say security wait times have hit the highest levels in the agency’s history.

Even with President Trump’s order to pay TSA agents, the question remains: how many trained officers have already walked away, and how long will it take to rebuild an agency that was already stretched thin?

As the shutdown negotiations continue, one thing is clear — the next time you breeze through airport security in 15 minutes, it might be worth remembering the people who make that possible, and how fragile the system can be when they’re not there.
