Crypto ATM Scams Cost Americans $333 Million — What You Need to Know

March 31, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

Did you know the average victim of a crypto ATM scam is 73 years old and loses roughly $17,000 in a single transaction? That’s not a hypothetical — it’s a real statistic from fraud investigators in Manchester, New Hampshire, and it’s part of a national crisis that’s finally forcing lawmakers to act.

Key Takeaways:

  • The FBI reported $333.5 million in crypto ATM scam losses from January to November 2025 — up from $250 million in 2024.
  • Indiana became the first state to ban crypto kiosks outright, sidelining roughly 800 machines.
  • The global crypto ATM count has dropped to 38,928 after a net loss of 597 machines in Q1 2026.
  • New Hampshire, West Virginia, and Arizona are advancing new consumer protection legislation.

Crypto ATM Fraud Is Surging Across the United States

The numbers are staggering. According to the FBI, fraudsters stole $333.5 million from Americans through bitcoin ATM scams between January and November 2025. That figure represents a 33% increase over the roughly $250 million lost in 2024, which itself was more than double the previous year’s total.

A bureau spokesperson described the trend as a “clear and constant rise” that is “not slowing down.”

There are over 30,000 crypto ATMs operating across the United States — machines that allow users to insert cash and send it to a digital wallet anywhere in the world in just minutes. Once the transaction is completed, the money is nearly impossible to recover, making these kiosks an ideal tool for criminals.

“Requesting crypto is now the No. 1 preferred method of criminals,” said Amy Nofziger, AARP’s director of fraud victim support.

The scams typically follow a familiar playbook: victims receive calls or messages claiming they’re in legal trouble, their bank accounts have been compromised, or they owe urgent debts. They’re then directed to a nearby crypto ATM and instructed to feed in cash — which is immediately routed to the scammer’s wallet.

So how are states fighting back?

States Fight Back: Bans, Bills, and New Regulations

The legislative response is accelerating in 2026, with states taking dramatically different approaches to the problem.

Indiana made history in March by becoming the first state to ban crypto kiosks entirely. An emergency declaration shut down approximately 800 machines statewide, citing their outsized role in consumer fraud.

New Hampshire is pursuing regulation rather than prohibition. On March 26, the state Senate passed Senate Bill 482 on a voice vote, the state’s first attempt to regulate crypto ATMs. The bill now heads to the House, where some of its toughest provisions could face opposition.

The urgency behind the New Hampshire bill is clear. Manchester Police fraud investigator Ray Lamy testified that his unit handled 66 fraud cases tied to just 22 crypto ATMs in the city between 2021 and 2025. Hampton Police Chief Alex Reno reported that residents of his town alone lost $2.6 million to scammers since late 2024, with 30 crypto ATMs operating within a 15-mile radius.

One case involved an 80-year-old woman who was trying to close an online retail account and was manipulated into bringing $17,000 in cash to a machine. She only realized she’d been robbed when a friend intervened.

Other states joining the push include:

  • West Virginia, where a crypto ATM bill is nearing completion in the legislature
  • Arizona, where officials have warned of a spike in bitcoin ATM scams targeting seniors
  • Washington, D.C., where the attorney general sued Athena Bitcoin, one of the nation’s largest crypto ATM operators, accusing it of “pocketing hundreds of thousands of dollars in undisclosed fees on the backs of scam victims”

The patchwork of state responses raises a critical question: is regulation enough, or will more states follow Indiana’s outright ban?

The regulatory crackdown is coinciding with a notable contraction in the crypto ATM market itself. According to data from Coin ATM Radar, 597 machines were removed from the global market in Q1 2026, bringing the worldwide total down to 38,928.

This consolidation means a handful of companies hold enormous influence over how these machines operate and what consumer protections they provide.

How Crypto ATM Scams Actually Work — and Why They’re So Effective

Understanding the mechanics of these scams reveals why they’re so devastatingly effective, particularly against older adults.

A CNN investigation documented what happened at a single crypto ATM in a Prescott, Arizona, convenience store. In just four days, four people were defrauded at the same machine:

  • Jeanne lost $18,000, feeding bills into the machine one by one
  • Patricia lost $3,000 hours later at the same spot
  • Heather lost $25,000 the next day
  • Lily lost nearly $8,000 two days after that

Since the previous year, at least a dozen victims lost a combined $118,000 at that single machine. Local police were aware of the pattern but were “all but powerless to stop the scammers.”

The scams exploit three psychological pressure points:

  1. Urgency — Victims are told they must act immediately to avoid arrest, account closure, or financial ruin
  2. Authority — Scammers impersonate government agencies, banks, or tech support companies
  3. Isolation — Victims are instructed not to tell anyone, especially bank employees or family members

The irreversibility of crypto transactions is the final element. Unlike wire transfers or credit card charges, there’s no chargeback mechanism and no fraud department to call.

In Essex County, Massachusetts, DA Tucker has managed to return funds to some crypto ATM scam victims, but such recoveries remain rare exceptions rather than the rule.

What This Means for You: Protecting Yourself and Your Family

Whether you use cryptocurrency or not, crypto ATM scams affect everyone — especially if you have elderly family members who might be targeted.

Here’s how to protect yourself:

  • No legitimate organization — not the IRS, not your bank, not a tech company — will ever ask you to pay via a crypto ATM
  • If someone creates urgency, hang up. Real emergencies don’t require you to visit a convenience store with cash
  • Talk to older relatives about these scams before they’re targeted. The average victim is 73 years old
  • Report suspicious activity to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov or call your local police department
  • If you’ve been victimized, contact your local district attorney’s office immediately — early reporting offers the best chance of recovery

The crypto ATM landscape is at a turning point. With $333 million lost in a single year, Indiana banning machines outright, and multiple states advancing new regulations, the era of unregulated crypto kiosks in convenience stores appears to be ending.

The real question is whether regulation will move fast enough to protect the next 73-year-old who walks into a store and encounters a machine that looks like an ordinary ATM — but isn’t.
