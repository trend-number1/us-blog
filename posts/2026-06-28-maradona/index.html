Maradona's Retrial: The Surprising Truth Behind a Legend's Death

June 28, 2026 12:03 Trend Insights

What if one of the greatest footballers who ever lived could have been saved? That is the question an Argentine court is still trying to answer more than five years after Maradona died, and the answer could send seven medical professionals to prison.

Key Takeaways

  • Diego Maradona died on November 25, 2020, at age 60 from acute heart failure and pulmonary edema.
  • A second trial of his medical team began on March 17, 2026, after the first collapsed in a mistrial.
  • Forensic experts found his heart weighed 503 grams — almost twice the normal size.
  • A verdict, originally expected in July, is now postponed to August at the earliest.

The story is no longer only about the Hand of God or two World Cup braces 40 years apart. In 2026, the Maradona name is in headlines for a courtroom drama that is testing whether Argentina’s justice system treats its idols like everyone else.

Why Maradona Is Trending Again in 2026

Diego Armando Maradona died on November 25, 2020, in a rental house on the outskirts of Buenos Aires. He was recovering from surgery to remove a chronic subdural hematoma — a blood clot on the brain — and never left the property alive.

His death plunged Argentina into collective grief so deep it briefly broke the COVID-19 restrictions then in place. Five years on, fans still flood his social accounts with messages like “Diego forever” and “We will always miss you.”

But grief has turned into a demand for justice. The Maradona family believes his death could have been prevented, and they want accountability so their idol can finally “rest in peace.”

That demand is what keeps his name in the news today. So what exactly happened in those final days?

The Medical Case: A Heart Heavier Than a Football

Here is the detail that stunned even seasoned observers. When forensic expert Carlos Mauricio Casinelli examined Maradona’s body, he found a heart weighing 503 grams — heavier than a regulation football and almost twice the weight of a normal human heart.

Casinelli, director of forensic medicine at the Scientific Police Superintendency, testified that Maradona likely “died in agony.” Fluid had been accumulating in his lungs for “at least 10 days” before death, and the enlarged heart would have caused suffering for at least 12 hours beforehand.

Prosecutors described his final days as a “horror theatre.” A panel of medical experts concluded the treatment he received at home was “deficient and reckless,” and that he “would have had a better chance of survival” in a proper medical facility.

The defense tells a different story. They argue Maradona had serious pre-existing conditions — including dilated cardiomyopathy, a disease that weakens the heart muscle and enlarges its chambers — and that his heart deteriorated suddenly and unavoidably.

Which version the court believes will decide everything. So who exactly is on trial?

Who Is on Trial and What They Face

Investigators classified the case as culpable homicide — a charge similar to involuntary manslaughter — arguing the accused knew how serious Maradona’s condition was but failed to act. In the second trial, prosecutors frame it as “homicide with eventual intent.”

The defendants include:

  1. Leopoldo Luque — his main medical adviser and neurosurgeon
  2. Agustina Cosachov — his psychiatrist
  3. Dahiana Gisela Madrid — his former nurse, who faces a separate trial
  4. Additional medical staffers who treated him at home

All have denied the allegations. If convicted, they face between 8 and 25 years in prison.

Reports have varied on the exact number of accused — initial coverage referenced seven people charged, while El País reported eight members of the medical team in the dock since April. What is undisputed is the central claim: that Maradona’s care fell catastrophically short.

That mistrial is one of the strangest twists in the entire saga. Here is why it happened.

The Mistrial That Restarted Everything

The first trial collapsed in May 2025 in spectacular fashion. One of its three judges, Julieta Makintach, resigned after allegedly allowing unauthorized filming in court for a documentary about the very trial she was overseeing.

It was found that she had taken part in a show about the case — a conflict serious enough to void the proceedings entirely and force a fresh start.

The retrial then opened on March 17, 2026, with eight members of the medical team back in the dock since April. A ruling that was scheduled for July has slipped to August at the earliest, a reminder that, as El País put it, the Argentine justice system “makes no exceptions for popular heroes regarding the pace at which it moves.”

So beyond the courtroom, why does any of this matter to you?

What This Means For You

Even if you have never watched a full match, the Maradona case carries lessons that travel far beyond football.

  • Fame doesn’t guarantee good care. One of the planet’s most famous athletes received treatment a panel called “deficient and reckless.” Advocating for proper medical settings matters for everyone.
  • Warning signs are easy to ignore. Fluid built in his lungs for roughly 10 days. Persistent symptoms after surgery deserve urgent attention, not a wait-and-see approach.
  • Legacy and accountability can coexist. Argentina is honoring its idol while still demanding answers — proof that admiration and hard questions are not mutually exclusive.

A common misconception is that this trial somehow tarnishes Maradona’s greatness on the pitch. It does not. The “Hand of God” goal is still debated 40 years later, and fans this year have compared Lionel Messi’s World Cup heroics directly to Maradona’s. The legend is intact; the trial is about justice, not genius.

The Verdict Still Ahead

More than five years after his death, Argentines know how Maradona’s heart failed and how large it had grown. The one question still unanswered is the one that matters most: could it have been prevented?

A verdict expected by August could finally provide closure — or reopen old wounds. Either way, the world will be watching, just as it always did when the number 10 had the ball.

Here is the question worth sitting with: when a hero dies in circumstances that “could have been avoided,” should the law treat that loss any differently than it would for anyone else?
