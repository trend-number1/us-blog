Chicken Alert: USDA Warns Shoppers in 9 States

June 29, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

If there’s a package of honey Dijon chicken breasts in your fridge right now, stop and check the label before you cook dinner. A nationwide chicken alert issued by the USDA could be sitting in your kitchen — and the problem isn’t something you can see, smell, or taste.

On Thursday, June 26, 2026, the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) issued a public health alert for certain raw boneless chicken breast products sold across nine states. The reason is deceptively simple: the wrong label was stuck on the back of the package, hiding the fact that the product contains eggs, one of the nine major food allergens.

Quick Summary

  • What: Private Selection Honey Dijon Boneless Skinless Chicken Breasts with Rib Meat (21 oz)
  • Why: Undeclared egg allergen due to an incorrect back label
  • Where: Kroger and Fred Meyer stores in 9 states
  • Best If Used By: June 28, 2026 — Lot code 15326A, Est. P-45288B
  • Action: Do not eat it. Throw it out or return it.

What Triggered This Chicken Alert

The affected items are 21-ounce vacuum-packed packages of Private Selection Honey Dijon Boneless Skinless Chicken Breasts with Rib Meat, produced on June 2, 2026.

According to FSIS, the issue surfaced in an unusually low-tech way. A retailer discovered that while the correct front label had been applied to the package, the wrong back label — the one carrying the ingredients statement — had been attached instead.

That mismatch meant the packaging failed to disclose that the product contains eggs. The retailer reported the problem to the producing establishment, which then notified FSIS.

Here’s the part many shoppers miss: a recall was not requested. Because the product is past its sell date and no longer available for purchase, FSIS issued a public health alert rather than a formal recall. The agency is still concerned that packages may be sitting in consumers’ refrigerators or freezers.

So how do you know if the chicken in your kitchen is part of this alert? The label details matter — let’s break them down.

How to Identify the Affected Product

Don’t rely on the brand name alone. Use the exact identifiers FSIS published to confirm whether your package is involved.

  • Product: Private Selection Honey Dijon Boneless Skinless Chicken Breasts with Rib Meat
  • Package size: 21-ounce vacuum-packed
  • Production date: June 2, 2026
  • Best If Used By date: June 28, 2026
  • Lot code: 15326A
  • Establishment number: P-45288B (printed on the packaging)

Here’s a quick comparison to clarify what this alert is — and isn’t.

DetailThis AlertA Typical Recall

The takeaway: even without a formal recall, the safety guidance is identical — don’t take the risk.

Now, the obvious question. If you don’t have an egg allergy, does this chicken alert even matter to you? The honest answer might surprise you.

What This Means For You

For most people, eggs are a harmless and common ingredient. But for someone with an egg allergy, undeclared eggs in a chicken product can trigger a reaction ranging from mild hives to severe anaphylaxis.

That’s why an undeclared allergen is treated as a genuine food-safety hazard, not a paperwork slip. The danger lives in what the label fails to say.

As of the alert’s issuance, there were no confirmed reports of adverse reactions or illnesses linked to these products. FSIS noted that anyone concerned about a possible reaction should contact a health care provider.

Here are the steps to take right now:

  1. Check your fridge and freezer for the 21-oz Private Selection Honey Dijon chicken breasts.
  2. Match the identifiers — lot code 15326A and establishment number P-45288B.
  3. Do not consume the product if it matches.
  4. Discard it or return it to the store where you bought it.
  5. Call your doctor if you’ve eaten it and have an egg allergy or feel unwell.

Even though the “Best If Used By” date has passed, frozen chicken can linger for weeks or months — which is exactly why FSIS keeps these alerts active.

The 9 States in the Distribution Zone

The products were shipped to Kroger and Fred Meyer retail stores in the following nine states:

  • Alabama
  • Alaska
  • Idaho
  • Illinois
  • Indiana
  • Kentucky
  • Tennessee
  • Oregon
  • Washington

If you shop at Kroger or Fred Meyer in any of these states, it’s worth a two-minute check of your freezer. Distribution lists like this are a reminder of how far a single mislabeled production run can travel across the country.

A Common Misconception About “Alerts” vs. “Recalls”

Many shoppers assume a “public health alert” is less serious than a “recall.” In practice, that’s a misreading.

The difference is largely about timing and availability, not severity. FSIS issues an alert (instead of a recall) when the affected product is no longer being sold — as is the case here, since the best-by date has passed. The hazard itself is treated just as seriously.

In other words: the absence of a recall is a quirk of logistics, not a green light to eat the chicken.

And not every “chicken alert” is even about food. In one widely shared story, a California woman named Victoria Mejia got a Blink door-camera notification warning of a “brown chicken” in her yard — only to realize the footage showed herself, with her clipped-back hair sticking up and apparently resembling a bird. She posted the clip to TikTok and, in her words, “openly laughed out loud.” It’s a lighthearted contrast to the USDA’s very real warning.

The Bottom Line

This chicken alert is a textbook example of how a small labeling error becomes a national food-safety issue. The chicken itself isn’t contaminated — but for the millions of Americans managing food allergies, an undeclared egg can be just as dangerous as any pathogen.

The fix on your end takes about two minutes: check the label, match the lot code, and discard or return anything that fits the description.

So here’s the question worth asking the next time you unpack groceries: how often do you actually read the back label — and would you have caught this one?
