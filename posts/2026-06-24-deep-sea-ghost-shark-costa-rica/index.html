Deep-Sea Ghost Shark Discovered Off Costa Rica

June 24, 2026 18:03 Trend Insights

Imagine a fish with a ghostly face that has drifted through the ocean’s darkest layers for hundreds of millions of years — and we only just learned it exists.

That is exactly what scientists announced this month off the Pacific coast of Costa Rica. A deep-sea ghost shark new to science has been formally described, and it is forcing researchers to admit how little we actually know about our own oceans.

Quick Summary

  • A new species, Rhinochimaera costaricana, was identified off Costa Rica’s Pacific coast.
  • It belongs to the “ghost shark” family — long-nosed chimaeras related to, but distinct from, true sharks.
  • Specimens were found at depths of 390 to 787 meters (about 1,280 to 2,580 feet).
  • The discovery was published this month in the journal Zootaxa.

A New Ghost Shark Hiding in Costa Rican Waters

The newly described deep-sea ghost shark off Costa Rica has been named Rhinochimaera costaricana. It belongs to a little-known group of cartilaginous fishes called long-nosed chimaeras, often nicknamed ghost sharks.

Although they are related to sharks and rays, chimaeras form a completely separate branch of fish. That lineage has existed for hundreds of millions of years, making these animals living windows into a prehistoric world.

The discovery was published this month in the scientific journal Zootaxa. The work came from researchers at Costa Rica’s Fisheries and Aquaculture Institute (INCOPESCA), the University of Costa Rica, and Brazil’s Federal University of Pará.

What makes this find so striking is where it happened: not in some remote, unexplored trench halfway across the planet, but in the national waters of a country famous for its biodiversity. So how did scientists confirm it was truly new?

How Scientists Confirmed a New Species

Naming a new species is not as simple as spotting an unfamiliar fish. The team built its case carefully, using both physical and genetic evidence.

Researchers based their description on three male specimens collected from Costa Rica’s Pacific waters between 2000 and 2023. The fish were found at depths ranging from 390 to 787 meters — far below where most recreational divers will ever venture.

To prove the fish was distinct, scientists compared 49 body measurements from these specimens against data from more than 90 individuals representing the three previously recognized species of Rhinochimaera.

The Costa Rican fish stood out with a unique combination of traits:

  • A shorter snout than its relatives
  • A taller first dorsal fin and spine
  • A wider gap between the two dorsal fins
  • Fewer tubercles along the tail

DNA analysis sealed the conclusion. Genetic comparisons showed clear differences between the Costa Rican fish and its known cousins, confirming that this was indeed a separate species.

That second column points to a separate but equally fascinating story — and it shows Costa Rica is becoming a hotspot for surprising shark science.

Costa Rica’s Other Shark Surprise: The “Golden Ghost”

Just months before the ghost shark announcement, Costa Rica produced another jaw-dropping marine headline.

In August 2024, sport fishers near Tortuguero National Park on the Caribbean coast hauled up a roughly 2-meter (6.6-foot) nurse shark (Ginglymostoma cirratum) at a depth of about 37 meters. Instead of the species’ usual brown, cookie-dough color, this one was a vivid orange — with eerie white eyes that appeared to have no visible iris.

The fisher, Juan Pablo, photographed and measured the animal before releasing it unharmed. The images, shared by the eco-tourism company Parismina Domus Day, quickly went viral.

Marine biologists led by Marioxis Macías-Cuyare of Brazil’s Federal University of Rio Grande investigated. Their diagnosis, published in Marine Biodiversity in August 2025, was albino-xanthochromism — a rare combination of albinism (lack of dark pigment) and xanthism (excess yellow pigment).

Remarkably, the condition did not appear to hurt the shark’s survival, even though its bright color stripped away the camouflage nurse sharks normally rely on to blend into the seabed.

Two extraordinary sharks, two coasts, one small country. The pattern raises a bigger question about what else is down there.

Why This Discovery Matters More Than You Think

It is easy to file “new fish discovered” under trivia. But the Costa Rican ghost shark carries a heavier message.

Scientists explicitly framed the find as evidence of how much marine biodiversity remains unexplored — even in waters that nations have fished and studied for decades. If a species this old and distinctive went unnoticed in Costa Rica’s Pacific, the deep ocean almost certainly hides far more.

A common misconception is that the age of “great discoveries” ended long ago. In reality, the deep sea remains one of the least-mapped environments on Earth, and cartilaginous fishes like chimaeras are among the hardest to study because they live so far below the surface.

What this means for you: the next time you hear that the oceans are “fully explored,” remember Rhinochimaera costaricana. Understanding species like this is essential for marine conservation, because you cannot protect what you do not yet know exists.

There is also a practical takeaway. Both Costa Rican discoveries reached scientists thanks to observant people — fishers and tour operators who photographed something unusual and shared it. If you ever encounter strange wildlife, clear photos, measurements, and a precise location can genuinely contribute to research.

Key Takeaways

To pull it all together:

  1. A genuinely new ghost shark species, Rhinochimaera costaricana, now joins the scientific record from Costa Rica’s Pacific deep.
  2. Rigorous methods — 49 body measurements plus DNA — confirmed it as distinct from its three known relatives.
  3. Costa Rica is a shark-science hotspot, also producing the rare “golden ghost” orange nurse shark in 2025.
  4. The deep sea is still a frontier, and citizen observations help unlock its secrets.

The discovery of a deep-sea ghost shark off Costa Rica is a reminder that the unknown is closer than we assume. If a prehistoric fish can hide in the waters of a well-studied coastline, what else is waiting just beyond the reach of our lights?

So here’s a question worth sitting with: if we have barely scratched the surface of the ocean, what discoveries might the next decade of deep-sea exploration bring — and are we doing enough to protect those creatures before we even meet them?
