Isis Sio in Coma: 19-Year-Old Boxer Fighting for Her Life After First-Round KO

March 23, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

The boxing world is holding its breath after 19-year-old Isis Sio was placed in a medically induced coma following a devastating first-round knockout in San Bernardino, California on Saturday night, March 21, 2026.

Key Takeaways:

  • Isis Sio, 19, is in a medically induced coma at Loma Linda University Health after being knocked out by Jocelyn Camarillo
  • The stoppage came at 1:18 of the first round at ProBoxTV’s event in San Bernardino
  • Sio had suffered a prior knockout loss just 50 days earlier in January
  • California rules mandate a minimum 30-day medical suspension after a knockout
  • The boxing community is rallying around Sio and her family

What Happened to Isis Sio on Saturday Night

Sio was facing Jocelyn Camarillo (6-0) in the opening bout of ProBoxTV’s card at the National Orange Show Event Center in San Bernardino. The main event featured Lester Martinez winning the interim WBC super middleweight title against Immanuwel Aleem, but the undercard fight between Sio and Camarillo has overshadowed everything.

Camarillo, a 21-year-old three-time national amateur champion, trapped Sio against the ropes and unleashed a sustained barrage. After landing two body shots that forced Sio to step back and cover up, Camarillo pounced with a series of consecutive head punches that left Sio unconscious.

The fight was stopped at 1:18 of the first round.

Sio remained unconscious for several minutes after the stoppage and was seen convulsing as she was stretchered out of the arena. She was immediately rushed to Loma Linda University Health medical center for emergency treatment.

The speed and severity of the incident have left the boxing community shaken — and asking difficult questions about fighter safety.

Isis Sio’s Brief Professional Career

The young fighter from North Dakota turned professional in September 2025 and had compiled a record of 1-3 in her short career. Her lone victory came via unanimous decision against Katelyn Radtka in November 2025.

Before turning professional, Sio had been profiled by local media as a rising talent. KFYR-TV highlighted her as the only active female professional boxer in North Dakota, and The Sheridan Press covered her journey into the sport, where she found both her passion and love in the ring.

What makes this situation even more troubling is the timeline between her last two fights. Sio was stopped by body shots on January 30 in Long Beach, California. She returned to the ring just 50 days later — meeting the bare minimum of California State Athletic Commission rules, which mandate a minimum 30-day medical suspension after a knockout, as determined by the ringside doctor.

ProBoxTV and the Boxing Community Respond

ProBoxTV released a statement on Sunday confirming Sio’s condition.

“On behalf of CEO Garry Jonas and the entire ProBox family, we are praying for a speedy recovery for Isis Sio,” the promotion posted on social media. “Ms. Sio is currently in a medically induced coma. Our thoughts are with her and her family at this very difficult time.”

An official briefed on Sio’s condition told BoxingScene on Sunday: “It’s not good.”

Camarillo, who had never previously scored a knockout in her six professional fights, also addressed the situation after the bout.

“Glory to God. I came prepared and gave everything,” Camarillo said. “I am grateful for the moment, but my heart is with Isis. I am keeping her in my prayers and wishing her a full recovery.”

The emotional weight of the situation was evident. In an interview with reporter Elie Seckbach immediately after the fight — before Sio’s critical condition was known — Camarillo described the moment as bittersweet, expressing both pride in her performance and concern for her opponent.

The Bigger Question: Women’s Boxing Safety Standards

This incident brings renewed scrutiny to fighter safety protocols in professional boxing, particularly at the lower weight classes and on smaller cards.

Several factors deserve closer examination:

  • Quick turnaround after a knockout: Sio fought just 50 days after being stopped, meeting only the minimum suspension period
  • Matchmaking concerns: A 1-2 fighter with two stoppage losses was matched against an undefeated, technically superior opponent with extensive amateur credentials
  • Medical screening: Questions about whether pre-fight medical evaluations adequately assessed Sio’s readiness to compete after her January stoppage
  • Event-level medical preparedness: While Sio received immediate medical attention at ringside, the severity of her condition raises questions about the resources available at smaller events

Women’s boxing has grown exponentially in recent years, with stars like Katie Taylor and Claressa Shields bringing unprecedented attention to the sport. But that growth also means more fighters are entering the professional ranks, and the sport’s safety infrastructure must keep pace.

The California State Athletic Commission oversees some of the strictest regulations in combat sports. Yet even within that framework, a 19-year-old fighter with a recent knockout on her record was cleared to compete — and is now in a medically induced coma.

What This Means for the Sport Going Forward

Isis Sio’s situation is a stark reminder that boxing carries inherent, life-threatening risks at every level. While high-profile tragedies like those involving professional fighters tend to generate the most headlines, the fighters at the beginning of their careers — with limited experience and developing defensive skills — may be among the most vulnerable.

The conversation about mandatory rest periods, enhanced medical screening, and matchmaking oversight is not new. But each incident like this adds urgency to the call for reform.

For now, the boxing world waits for updates on Sio’s condition at Loma Linda University Health. Fighters, promoters, and fans alike have taken to social media to send prayers and well-wishes to the young North Dakota fighter and her family.

As Camarillo herself said: “We hope the girl is okay. We send all our prayers to her and her entire team.”

Will this be the wake-up call that prompts meaningful changes in how boxing protects its youngest and most vulnerable fighters? The answer may depend on whether the sport’s governing bodies treat this as an isolated tragedy — or a systemic problem that demands action.
