Sonia Sotomayor's Supreme Court Dissents Are Shaping the National Conversation

April 2, 2026 00:03 Trend Insights

When the Supreme Court refuses to act, Sonia Sotomayor refuses to stay silent. In the span of just two weeks, the senior liberal justice has issued fiery dissent after fiery dissent — calling out her colleagues for leaving what she calls clear injustices in place. Her words are resonating far beyond the courtroom.

Key Takeaways:

  • Sotomayor dissented sharply after the Supreme Court declined to review the James Skinner case, where a co-defendant convicted of the same crime was freed years earlier.
  • She accused the Court of failing to “enforce its own precedents” under the Brady doctrine.
  • She also dissented the prior week in the Rodney Reed case, warning Texas may execute a man without DNA evidence being fully examined.
  • Sotomayor received the Marshall-Wythe Medallion, William & Mary Law School’s highest honor, in February 2026.
  • She continues to champion civic engagement, telling ABC News that “democracy does not survive unless the people in it understand that they are the agents of law.”

Sotomayor Blasts SCOTUS Over the Skinner Case

On March 30, 2026, the Supreme Court declined to review the case of James Skinner, sentenced to life in prison for the 1998 murder of 16-year-old pizza delivery driver Eric Walber in Louisiana. Sotomayor, joined only by Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, issued a blistering dissent.

The core of her argument? The Court had already addressed this very evidence — and reached a different conclusion for a different defendant.

Skinner’s co-defendant, Michael Wearry, was convicted of the same crime and sentenced to death. In 2016, the Supreme Court vacated Wearry’s conviction because the prosecution violated its duty under the Brady v. Maryland doctrine to disclose favorable evidence to the defense. That evidence included two key eyewitness accounts central to both cases.

Yet when Skinner raised essentially the same constitutional claim about the same buried evidence, the Court refused to hear it.

“Equal justice under law, the phrase engraved on the front of this Court’s building, requires that two co-defendants, convicted of the same crime, who raised essentially the same constitutional claims, receive the same answer from the courts,” Sotomayor wrote.

The result, she warned, is devastating: Skinner risks spending the rest of his life in prison while Wearry walks free.

So what changed between these two rulings? That’s the question Sotomayor wants answered — and her colleagues’ silence speaks volumes.

A Pattern of Powerful Dissents

The Skinner dissent didn’t come in isolation. Just one week earlier, Sotomayor had penned another protest — this time in the case of Rodney Reed, a Texas death-row inmate.

In that case, she lamented the Court’s refusal to intervene, writing that the denial means Texas will likely execute Reed without ever knowing whether his or another person’s DNA is on the murder weapon. The case has drawn national attention from innocence advocates for years.

These back-to-back dissents illustrate a pattern that has defined Sotomayor’s tenure on the bench: she uses dissents not just as legal arguments, but as moral appeals to the public.

Her dissents have earned her standing ovations — literally. When she appeared on the Late Show with Stephen Colbert after her 21-page immigration dissent in 2025, the studio audience erupted in applause as Colbert read her words aloud.

But Sotomayor pushed back against partisan cheerleading, even from a friendly audience. “I want you to read it, and I want you to read the concurring opinion,” she told Colbert. “Too many people hear what a Supreme Court decision is, and they come to immediate conclusions based on what only their personal feelings are. You shouldn’t do that.”

Beyond the Bench: Honors and Advocacy

Sotomayor’s influence extends well beyond her written opinions. On February 20, 2026, William & Mary Law School presented her with the Marshall-Wythe Medallion, the institution’s highest honor.

The medallion recognizes members of the legal community who have demonstrated exceptional accomplishment in law. Sotomayor joined a distinguished list of past recipients that includes:

  • Justice Elena Kagan
  • Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg
  • Justice Sandra Day O’Connor (former W&M Chancellor)
  • Justice Antonin Scalia
  • Justice Thurgood Marshall

“Justice Sotomayor has shaped the law across every dimension of her career,” said Dean A. Benjamin Spencer. “Her legacy is not only one of jurisprudence, but also of leadership and inspiration to future generations.”

During a post-dinner fireside chat with faculty and students, Sotomayor spoke candidly about her career — from her years as a federal prosecutor to her roles as a trial and appellate judge, and ultimately as a Supreme Court justice.

She has also channeled her platform into children’s literature. Her illustrated book “Just Shine!” is a tribute to her late mother, Celina, who championed kindness. It’s part of Sotomayor’s broader mission to make the justice system — and civic participation — accessible to young people.

Democracy’s ‘Agents of Law’

In an interview with ABC News, Sotomayor offered a view of American democracy that was cautiously optimistic — but with a clear warning attached.

“Democracy does not survive unless the people in it understand that they are the agents of law, that they’re the ones that decide the world they live in,” she told anchor Linsey Davis. “Is it at risk? It can be if people take our democracy for granted.”

But she said she finds hope in what she sees around the country.

“I see lots of people taking stands and getting up and speaking and people trying to affect change,” she said. “As long as I continue to see that, then I have hope.”

Her remarks aligned, perhaps surprisingly, with those of conservative Justice Amy Coney Barrett, who said at a New York City event: “I think the Constitution is alive and well.”

The convergence of liberal and conservative justices on this point suggests a shared institutional concern: that the Court’s legitimacy depends on public trust and engagement, regardless of ideological disagreements on individual cases.

What This Means for You

Sotomayor’s dissents may not change the outcome of the cases she’s protesting. The Court has spoken — or rather, declined to speak. But her words serve a different purpose.

Dissents are roadmaps for the future. Some of the most famous dissents in Supreme Court history — from Justice Harlan’s lone dissent in Plessy v. Ferguson to Justice Ginsburg’s dissent in Shelby County v. Holder — eventually became the foundation for new legal standards.

Here’s what you can take away right now:

  1. Read the opinions yourself. As Sotomayor herself urges, don’t rely on headlines alone. Full opinions are available on the Supreme Court’s website.
  2. Understand the Brady doctrine. When prosecutors withhold evidence favorable to the defense, it undermines the entire justice system — and the Skinner case shows enforcement remains inconsistent.
  3. Stay engaged. Sotomayor’s core message is that democracy requires active participants, not passive observers.

Whether you agree with her jurisprudence or not, Sotomayor is forcing a conversation that the Court’s majority would rather avoid: What does equal justice actually look like in practice?

That question isn’t going away. And as long as Sotomayor has a seat on the bench, neither is she.
