Cheyenne Water System Bacteria: The Data Center Link

July 5, 2026 18:03 Trend Insights

What if the biggest threat to your city’s water supply wasn’t a factory or a farm, but a building full of computer servers?

That is exactly the question residents of Cheyenne, Wyoming are asking. The Cheyenne water system bacteria issue has exposed an uncomfortable truth about the AI data center boom: the same buildout driving up electricity and water demand is now showing up as a documented local water-quality problem.

Quick Summary

  • A rare, metal-resistant bacterium called Cupriavidus gilardii contaminated Cheyenne’s wastewater reclamation system in February 2026.
  • The city’s Board of Public Utilities (BOPU) traced it to Goat Systems LLC, the entity Meta uses to build its data center campus.
  • The contamination forced two reclamation facilities offline for months of cleaning.
  • The company’s sewer discharge privileges were “immediately and permanently terminated.”

What Actually Happened in Cheyenne

In late February 2026, lab staff at the Cheyenne Board of Public Utilities detected an “unusual bacterium” during a routine wastewater sample study.

The culprit was identified as Cupriavidus gilardii, a bacterium commonly found in soil environments. What makes it unusual is its high resistance to metals — a trait associated with certain industrial applications involving metal reduction processes, including advanced manufacturing systems.

The discovery forced the city to shut down its wastewater reclamation system shortly after it came online for the season. Both the Dry Creek and Crow Creek reclamation facilities were idled for months of cleaning.

Here is the detail that turned this into a scandal: the contamination happened in February, but BOPU did not publicly disclose it until July 2, 2026 — roughly four months later. That delay left residents demanding answers.

So who was responsible? For weeks, nobody would say. That silence only made the story bigger.

The Data Center Connection

City sanitation experts went to work immediately to find the source. A check-in with the Wyoming Department of Environmental Quality didn’t yield one, but tracing eventually led investigators to a single local industrial user.

That user was Goat Systems LLC — the corporate entity Meta uses to build its roughly 800,000-square-foot data center campus in Cheyenne’s High Plains Business Park. The project was known during development as “Project Cosmo.”

According to the BOPU, the bacterium entered the system through wastewater generated during a “fill-and-flush operation” — essentially, the purging of a closed-loop cooling system. Goat Systems LLC subsequently received a violation after the bacteria interfered with public utilities.

Even before the company was named, experts saw it coming. “It would be likely that a newer industry would be responsible,” said Jonathan Brant, a University of Wyoming associate professor of civil and architectural engineering who specializes in water treatment. “Data center discharges would be the first place I would look.”

The board’s response was blunt: the company’s discharge privileges were “immediately and permanently terminated.” The city is also temporarily pausing data center discharges more broadly while it sorts out the fallout.

But this isn’t just a Cheyenne story — and that’s where it gets bigger.

A Pattern, Not a One-Off

Here’s the insight that should give every community near a data center pause: Cheyenne wasn’t alone.

Two data center water incidents surfaced within roughly six weeks of each other in 2026, in states with nothing else in common:

LocationDate SurfacedCompany LinkedWater Impact
Cheyenne, WyomingJuly 2, 2026Meta contractor (Goat Systems)Rare bacterium; reclamation system offline for months
Morgan County, GeorgiaMay 20, 2026Meta data centerResidents report brown, undrinkable tap water

On May 20, 2026, U.S. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez held up jars of brown well water at a congressional hearing — testimony tied to a Meta data center in Morgan County, Georgia, where residents say their tap water has turned undrinkable.

The common thread is the AI infrastructure buildout. What was once framed as a resource-competition story — data centers using enormous amounts of water for cooling — is now becoming a water-quality story with documented contamination events.

What This Means for You

Should you be worried about your drinking water? The honest answer requires nuance.

According to the BOPU, infections from Cupriavidus gilardii are extremely rare. The bacterium may pose health risks primarily to immunocompromised individuals and the elderly through direct exposure. This was also a reclaimed/reuse water system — not the drinking-water supply — which is an important distinction.

That said, here are practical steps if you live near industrial or data center development:

  1. Watch for public notices. Boil-water advisories and utility bulletins are your first line of defense.
  2. Know your water source. Understand whether your community uses reclaimed water for irrigation or other purposes.
  3. Ask questions at public meetings. Cheyenne residents used public records requests to force transparency — a tool available in most jurisdictions.
  4. Pay attention to who discharges into your sewer system. Industrial users are legally prohibited from discharging hazardous substances into sanitary sewers.

Addressing a Common Misconception

Many people assume data centers are “clean” tech — just servers humming in a warehouse. In reality, the cooling systems that keep those servers from overheating rely on closed-loop water circuits that must periodically be flushed. When that flush water carries unusual contaminants into a municipal system, the consequences can idle critical infrastructure for months.

The bacterium’s metal resistance is the clue that ties it to industrial processes. This is not a case of ordinary sewage — it’s a signature of advanced manufacturing and industrial cooling.

The Bottom Line

The Cheyenne water system bacteria issue is a wake-up call. A rare, metal-resistant bacterium linked to a Meta data center contractor knocked out a city’s wastewater reclamation system for months and left residents in the dark for a third of a year.

As AI infrastructure spreads into small and mid-sized communities across the country, the question is no longer just how much water these facilities consume — but what they put back.

Would your community even know if its water system had been contaminated four months ago? That’s the question Cheyenne is still answering — and it may be one worth asking where you live, too.
