Josh Naylor's Surprising Slow Start: Why the Mariners Shouldn't Panic Yet

April 14, 2026 06:03 Trend Insights

A .104 batting average from your $92.5 million offseason centerpiece is enough to make any fanbase reach for the panic button. But what if the numbers hiding beneath that ugly surface tell a completely different story?

That is exactly the situation unfolding with Josh Naylor and the Seattle Mariners in the early weeks of the 2026 MLB season. And on April 13, Naylor may have fired the first shot of a turnaround, launching a homer against the Houston Astros that scored Cal Raleigh and Julio Rodriguez in a much-needed offensive burst.

Key Takeaways

  • Naylor’s .104 batting average through 13 games masks a Statcast expected batting average of .230 — over 100 points higher
  • His barrel rate (7.5%), sweet-spot rate (37.5%), and squared-up rate (89th percentile) all look healthy
  • The five-year, $92.5 million contract includes a full no-trade clause, signaling long-term commitment
  • A three-run homer on April 13 against Houston could mark the start of his regression to the mean

Josh Naylor and the $92.5 Million Bet

When the Mariners signed Naylor to a five-year, $92.5 million free-agent contract this past offseason — the richest deal for a position player under president of baseball operations Jerry Dipoto — it was a statement move. The previous high under Dipoto had been a two-year, $24 million deal for Mitch Garver. The only larger contract in franchise history was Robinson Cano’s 10-year, $240 million mega-deal during the 2013-14 offseason.

The logic was sound. After arriving via trade deadline deal last July, Naylor was electric in a Mariners uniform, slashing .299/.341/.490 with nine home runs, 33 RBI, and 19 stolen bases across 54 regular-season games. He carried that momentum deep into October, posting a staggering .967 OPS with three home runs and five RBI in 12 postseason starts during Seattle’s memorable run.

“Ensuring that Josh remains a Mariner for the long haul was a priority for us,” Dipoto said when the deal was announced. “Josh’s intelligence, intensity, toughness, and competitiveness show up every day. He’s simply a winner.”

ESPN’s Jeff Passan echoed the sentiment, noting that Naylor’s “energy was infectious” and that “Seattle didn’t want to lose it.” So what happened when the 2026 season began?

Five hits in 48 at-bats is brutal. The Mariners’ offense as a whole has been grinding through early-season struggles, and seeing their marquee signing at the bottom of the leaderboard only amplifies the frustration.

Sports Illustrated labeled it a “miserable start,” and Sporting News ran a piece dissecting what it called the Mariners’ “potential $92 million mistake.” Fantasy baseball analysts at FantasyPros listed Naylor among their top buy-low candidates, while Yahoo Sports placed him among the three unluckiest hitters in MLB alongside Kansas City’s Bobby Witt and Cleveland’s Kyle Manzardo.

But is it really a crisis — or is it just baseball’s cruelest early-season variance?

What Statcast Actually Says About Naylor

This is where the conversation needs to slow down, because the process and the results are telling two very different stories.

According to Statcast, Naylor’s expected batting average sits at .230 — more than 100 points above his actual .104. His expected wOBA also sits well above his real-world production. That kind of gap between expected and actual performance is a textbook indicator of bad luck, not bad hitting.

Here is what the batted-ball data reveals:

  • Barrel rate: 7.5% — right in line with his career norms
  • Sweet-spot rate: 37.5% — actually elevated from his typical mark
  • Squared-up rate: 89th percentile among all MLB hitters
  • Strikeout rate: 81st percentile (meaning he’s striking out less than most)

That statistical profile does not belong to a hitter in genuine freefall. The strikeout rate is especially telling: with only eight punchouts in 52 plate appearances, Naylor is making contact and putting the ball in play. The hits simply are not falling — yet.

As Sports Illustrated’s analysis concluded, this looks far more like a “nasty patch of early-season baseball nonsense” than any mechanical breakdown or decline.

The Homer That Could Change Everything

On April 13 against the Houston Astros, Naylor provided a tangible sign that the regression to the mean may be arriving. He crushed a line-drive home run to right-center field, driving in both Cal Raleigh and Julio Rodriguez to give the Mariners a crucial offensive spark.

It was his first homer of the 2026 season, and the timing mattered. The Mariners’ offense has been a persistent concern. As The Seattle Times detailed, the numbers across the entire lineup have told a troubling early-season story. Julio Rodriguez himself has been battling through his own early struggles, though he publicly expressed confidence that he would overcome them.

A breakout from Naylor — one that the data strongly suggests is coming — could be exactly what unlocks the rest of Seattle’s lineup. When the middle of the order starts producing, the ripple effect tends to lift everyone around it.

What This Means for Fantasy Owners and Mariners Fans

If you are in a fantasy baseball league and someone in your group is ready to sell Naylor at a discount, this is the buy-low window analysts have been waiting for. FantasyPros specifically highlighted him as a buy-low target in their Week 3 analysis, and RotoBaller flagged him among risers to watch.

The combination of elite contact quality, low strikeout rate, and a massive gap between expected and actual stats makes this one of the clearest regression candidates in baseball right now.

For Mariners fans, the message is equally straightforward: patience. The front office committed $92.5 million and a full no-trade clause to this player because of what they saw during his 54-game audition and postseason heroics. Fourteen games of bad luck do not erase that.

Common misconception: A slow start to a season means a player has declined or the contract was a mistake. In reality, MLB’s 162-game schedule is designed to smooth out exactly this kind of variance. A player hitting .104 with elite underlying metrics is far less concerning than one hitting .250 with terrible batted-ball data.

The Bigger Picture for Seattle’s 2026 Season

The Mariners built their offseason around keeping Naylor as a foundational piece alongside Cal Raleigh, Julio Rodriguez, and their pitching rotation. That core is still intact, and nothing about Naylor’s early struggles suggests a structural problem.

If anything, the fact that his contact quality has remained strong — and even improved in some metrics like sweet-spot rate — should reassure the front office that their investment is sound. Baseball has a way of correcting these kinds of disconnects, and when it does, the correction can be swift and dramatic.

The question is not whether Josh Naylor will start hitting. The question is how quickly the rest of the Mariners’ offense joins him when he does. With Rodriguez working through his own early-season adjustments and Raleigh providing stability behind the plate, Seattle’s lineup has the talent to be dangerous.

Will the April 13 homer against Houston be the moment fans look back on as the turning point? Only time will tell — but the data says Naylor has been ready for a breakout all along. The results are finally starting to catch up.
