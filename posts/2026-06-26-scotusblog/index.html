SCOTUSblog: The Essential Guide to Supreme Court News

June 26, 2026 00:04 Trend Insights

Here’s a number that stopped a lot of people in 2007: 100,000 page views in a single day — for a blog about the Supreme Court. Not a celebrity scandal, not a sports score. Court opinions.

That blog was SCOTUSblog, and nearly two decades later it remains the single most-trusted independent source for understanding what the U.S. Supreme Court actually does. If you’ve ever wondered how reporters seem to explain a complex ruling within minutes of its release, the answer is often the same: they’re reading SCOTUSblog.

Quick Summary: SCOTUSblog is an independent law blog that tracks every case before the Supreme Court — from the early appeal stage through the final decision. It live blogs opinions as they’re announced, hosts expert symposiums, and keeps a full archive of legal documents. Founded in 2002, it’s now owned by The Dispatch.

Let’s break down why this one site became indispensable — and what it can do for you, even if you’ve never read a legal brief in your life.

What SCOTUSblog Actually Is

At its core, SCOTUSblog is a law blog written by lawyers, legal scholars, and law students who cover the Supreme Court of the United States (often abbreviated “SCOTUS”).

The site tracks cases through their entire journey. That means it follows a dispute from the certiorari stage — when the Court decides whether to even hear a case — all the way through the merits stage, where the justices issue their final ruling.

What sets it apart is the depth. SCOTUSblog comprehensively covers all of the cases argued before the Court and maintains an archive of the briefing and other documents in each one. For anyone who wants the primary sources rather than a hot take, that archive is gold.

It also does something most news outlets can’t: it live blogs as the Court announces opinions and grants cases. On a big decision day, that real-time feed is where the country’s legal community gathers.

Why does a blog beat traditional newsrooms here? That’s where the story gets interesting.

The Surprising Origin Story

SCOTUSblog didn’t start as a journalism project. It started as marketing.

The blog’s first post went up on October 1, 2002. It was founded by Supreme Court litigator Tom Goldstein and former litigator Amy Howe, originally as a way to promote their law firm, then known as Goldstein & Howe, P.C.

In other words, one of the most respected legal news sources in America began as a content strategy for a boutique law practice. There’s a lesson buried in that for any creator or business today.

A few milestones along the way:

  • February 7, 2005 — the blog moved to its current address.
  • 2005 — it was featured by BusinessWeek in their weekly blog recommendation.
  • 2007 — a companion wiki was added, though its features were later folded back into the blog itself.
  • June 2007 — the site announced it was about to hit its largest-ever daily readership.

That 2007 traffic spike wasn’t random. It coincided with the Supreme Court’s surprising reversal on June 29, 2007, when it unexpectedly announced it would hear the Guantanamo Bay detainees’ challenges. When the news broke, readers knew exactly where to go.

So who owns this influential platform now? The answer reflects how much the media landscape has shifted.

Who Owns SCOTUSblog Today

Ownership has changed hands as the site grew in stature. It was formerly sponsored by Bloomberg Law, and is now owned by The Dispatch.

That evolution — from a law firm’s side project, to a Bloomberg-backed property, to a Dispatch-owned publication — mirrors a broader trend in digital media: niche, expert-driven sites becoming valuable assets in their own right.

Here’s a quick look at how SCOTUSblog stacks up against the way most people get court news.

The takeaway? SCOTUSblog goes deep where most coverage stays shallow. And that depth has been on full display during the Court’s recent run of decisions.

A Snapshot of Recent Coverage

Late June is always intense at the Supreme Court, as the justices race to release opinions before their summer recess — and SCOTUSblog’s recent coverage captures that crush.

In one stretch, the site reported that the Court released opinions in Mullin v. Doe, Wolford v. Lopez, Mullin v. Al Otro Lado, and Monsanto Company v. Durnell on a single Thursday.

Its recent headlines also point to the range of issues in play, including:

  • A ruling that a former Louisiana inmate cannot sue prison officials in a religious dispute over long hair.
  • A decision siding with the government over the rights of green card holders accused of committing a crime.
  • The Court agreeing to hear a case on the ability to sue federal officials.
  • Analysis pieces like “When Congress ‘overrides’ the court” and explainers titled “What to know about the court’s five latest rulings.”

Notice the mix: breaking decisions and analysis that explains what those decisions mean. That combination is exactly what makes the site so useful — and it clears up a common misconception too.

A Common Misconception, Cleared Up

Many readers assume SCOTUSblog is an official government site or that it’s run by the Court itself. It isn’t. It’s an independent publication. The Court has no editorial control over it.

That independence is precisely why it can offer candid analysis and host symposiums with leading experts who debate cases from every angle.

What This Means For You

You don’t need a law degree to benefit from SCOTUSblog. Here’s how different readers can use it right now:

  1. Stay ahead of major rulings. On decision days, bookmark the live blog instead of refreshing a general news feed.
  2. Read the actual documents. Want to know what a case really argues? Pull the briefs straight from the case archive rather than relying on a 200-word summary.
  3. Understand the why. The analysis posts translate dense legal reasoning into plain English — useful for students, voters, and professionals alike.
  4. Track a case over time. Following a dispute that matters to you? The site lets you watch it move from the appeal stage to the final decision.

For students and aspiring lawyers especially, regularly reading the site is one of the best free ways to build legal literacy.

The Bottom Line

SCOTUSblog proves that a focused, expert-driven publication can outlast and out-inform far bigger newsrooms. From a single promotional post in 2002 to a daily destination for the legal world, it has become the connective tissue between the Supreme Court and the public.

The Court shapes American life in ways that touch everyone — from criminal justice to immigration to religious freedom. The question is whether you’ll wait for a watered-down headline, or go straight to the source that legal experts trust.

So here’s something to chew on: the next time a landmark ruling drops, where will you turn first — and how much of the real story will you actually understand?
