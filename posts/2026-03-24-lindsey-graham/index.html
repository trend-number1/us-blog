Lindsey Graham Under Fire: Iran War Rhetoric Sparks GOP Civil War and Reelection Crisis

March 24, 2026 12:03 Trend Insights

Has a single weekend ever done more damage to a sitting senator’s standing within his own party? Lindsey Graham’s call for the United States to seize Iran’s Kharg Island has ignited a firestorm — not from Democrats, but from fellow Republicans who say the South Carolina senator is treating American troops like “expendable cattle.”

Key Takeaways

  • Graham urged President Trump to “take Kharg Island” and let Iran’s regime “die on a vine,” comparing the operation to Iwo Jima.
  • Multiple MAGA-aligned Republicans — including Marjorie Taylor Greene and Nancy Mace — publicly denounced his rhetoric.
  • Graham’s approval rating in South Carolina sits at just 34 percent, making his 2026 reelection bid one of the most vulnerable in the Senate.
  • Trump has endorsed Graham and dispatched former campaign manager Chris LaCivita to advise his campaign.

Lindsey Graham’s Kharg Island Push Draws Bipartisan Outrage

Over the weekend, Senator Graham appeared on Fox News and made a bold pitch: he urged President Donald Trump to order a military strike on Kharg Island, a critical hub for Iran’s oil exports.

“We did Iwo Jima, we can do this,” Graham declared, calling on the administration to let the Iranian regime “die on a vine” by cutting off its economic lifeline.

The comparison to one of the bloodiest battles in American military history — where thousands of Marines lost their lives — did not sit well with many in his own party.

What followed was a rapid-fire series of rebukes from Republicans who have rarely broken ranks on foreign policy.

MAGA Republicans Turn on Graham

The backlash was swift and personal.

Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene labeled Graham “a psychotic neocon,” one of the sharpest insults a MAGA-aligned lawmaker can level at a colleague. Her comments reflected a broader shift within the Republican base away from interventionist foreign policy.

Rep. Nancy Mace, who represents South Carolina’s 1st Congressional District — Graham’s home state — went further. She said Graham should “be removed from the Situation Room” after his latest Iran war comments, a striking public rebuke of a senior senator by a fellow South Carolinian.

Rep. Anna Paulina Luna of Florida also denounced Graham’s rhetoric, accusing him of acting as though American service members are disposable.

The pattern is clear: Graham’s hawkish stance on Iran is increasingly out of step with the America First wing of the Republican Party, which has come to dominate GOP primary politics.

So how does this affect his political future?

A Reelection Campaign Already in Trouble

Graham’s Iran comments land at a particularly dangerous moment for his career. The four-term senator is running for a fifth six-year term in the November 2026 midterms, and the numbers are not encouraging.

A Winthrop University poll found Graham’s approval rating at just 34 percent in South Carolina — a state where he has served since 2003. For context, Governor Henry McMaster registered 42 percent and Senator Tim Scott came in at 41 percent in the same poll.

Those figures put Graham in a uniquely vulnerable position:

  • 34% approval is dangerously low for an incumbent senator in a deep-red state
  • He trails both major Republican officeholders in his own state by 7-8 points on favorability
  • 20 Republican Senate seats are up in 2026, giving Democrats a real opportunity to flip the chamber from its current 53-47 GOP majority
  • South Carolina, while reliably Republican in presidential elections (Trump won 58.23% in the last cycle), has shown it can turn on its own when dissatisfied

Graham’s best asset remains his relationship with President Trump. On Wednesday, Trump posted a glowing birthday message on Truth Social, calling Graham someone who “is always there when I need him” and urging South Carolinians to “help Lindsey have a BIG WIN.”

The president has also sent Chris LaCivita, his own former campaign manager, to advise Graham’s reelection effort — a signal that the Trump operation views the seat as worth protecting.

But will a presidential endorsement be enough when Graham’s own party base is calling him a warmonger?

What This Means for the 2026 Senate Map

Graham’s troubles matter far beyond South Carolina. With 33 Senate seats on the ballot in 2026 and 20 of those held by Republicans, every vulnerable incumbent changes the calculus.

Democrats need a net gain of just four seats to flip the Senate. If Graham’s seat becomes genuinely competitive, it forces the GOP to spend resources defending territory that should be safe — pulling money and attention away from offensive targets.

Key factors to watch:

  1. Primary challenge risk — Graham’s low approval and hawkish foreign policy stance could invite a credible primary challenger running on an America First platform
  2. Trump endorsement durability — While Trump’s backing is powerful, Graham’s Iran rhetoric directly contradicts the non-interventionist instincts of much of Trump’s base
  3. Democratic recruitment — A 34% approval rating in any state will attract ambitious challengers from the opposing party
  4. National security debate — If tensions with Iran escalate, Graham’s position could either be vindicated or further exposed depending on outcomes

The Wall Street Journal reported earlier this month on Graham’s broader quest to sell Trump on striking Iran, suggesting this is not a one-off statement but a sustained lobbying campaign. That persistence may ultimately determine whether he is seen as prescient or reckless.

The Bigger Picture: A Party Redefining Itself

Graham’s predicament illustrates a fundamental tension within the modern Republican Party. The senator built his career as a national security hawk in the mold of his late mentor, John McCain. For decades, that positioning was an asset.

But the GOP’s center of gravity has shifted. The party’s base now largely favors restraint abroad and skepticism toward military intervention. Graham’s Iwo Jima comparison didn’t just fall flat — it actively antagonized the voters he needs most.

At 70 years old and seeking a fifth term, Graham faces an uncomfortable question: has the party moved past him?

His Trump endorsement and LaCivita’s involvement suggest the institutional GOP is not ready to abandon him. But the grassroots fury over his Iran comments — from Greene, Mace, Luna, and countless voices online — tells a different story.

The next several months will reveal whether Lindsey Graham can navigate this divide, or whether his hawkish instincts have finally caught up with him in an America First era.

What do you think — can a presidential endorsement overcome a 34% approval rating and growing intraparty opposition? The 2026 midterms may provide the answer.
